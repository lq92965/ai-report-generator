Most status reporting fails for a boring reason: it's written for the person asking, not the person reading. The PM writes a paragraph about what they did this week. The director skims it for twelve seconds, finds no decision, and schedules a meeting to ask the question the report should have answered.

I've watched teams burn a full hour every Friday re-explaining things that were already in writing. The fix wasn't a new tool or a longer template. It was a one-page note with a fixed shape, sent at the same time every week, that makes one thing obvious: what changed, what's stuck, and what the reader needs to do.

Here's the format, why it works, and how to roll it out without turning it into another compliance exercise.

## Why most weekly updates get ignored

A status update competes with forty other emails for about fifteen seconds of attention. If the reader has to hunt for the point, they stop reading and ping you on Slack instead. The report didn't fail because it was too short. It failed because it had no shape.

Three failure modes show up again and again:

- **The diary.** A chronological list of activity ("met with design, reviewed specs, synced with legal"). It proves you were busy but tells the reader nothing about movement.
- **The bury.** The one thing that matters — a slipping date, a blocked dependency — sits in paragraph four under a cheerful subject line.
- **The orphan.** No owner, no ask, no date. The reader finishes and thinks, "So what do you want from me?"

The standing Friday note fixes all three by forcing a structure that maps to the questions a reader actually has.

## The six-block structure

Keep it to one screen. Every block has a job.

**1. Headline (one line).** The state of the project in plain language, plus a confidence signal. Not "Week 14 update." Something like: "On track for the May 9 launch; one open risk on the payments vendor."

**2. What moved since last week.** Three to five bullets, each tied to an outcome, not an activity. "Cut onboarding from five steps to three" beats "worked on onboarding." If nothing moved, say so — that's information too.

**3. What's stuck.** Name the blocker, the owner, and the date it needs to clear. One line each. This is the block that earns the format its keep; it's where you surface the thing you'd otherwise raise in a meeting.

**4. Decisions needed.** The reader's job. Phrase each as a question with a default: "Approve the vendor switch by Wednesday, or we stay on the current contract through Q3." A decision with no default invites a meeting. A decision with a default invites a reply.

**5. Numbers that matter.** Two or three metrics, each with last week's value and the trend. Resist the urge to dump a dashboard. Pick the numbers a reader would ask about if you left them out.

**6. Next week's focus.** Two or three bullets on what you'll actually do, so the reader can spot a mismatch between your priorities and theirs before it costs a sprint.

That's it. No preamble, no "hope you're well," no attachment with the real story inside.

## A filled-in example

> **Headline:** On track for May 9 launch. One risk on payments vendor — decision needed by Wednesday.
>
> **Moved:** Onboarding down to three steps; QA signed off on the new checkout flow; pricing page copy finalized.
>
> **Stuck:** Payments vendor contract still unsigned (owner: Dana, needs to clear by Apr 30). Analytics event schema waiting on data team.
>
> **Decisions needed:** Switch to backup payments vendor by Wednesday, or hold current contract through Q3? Default: hold.
>
> **Numbers:** Activation 41% (up from 38%); support tickets 22 (flat); checkout drop-off 12% (down from 15%).
>
> **Next week:** Load-test checkout; draft launch comms; close analytics schema.

Read that in fifteen seconds and you know exactly what to do. That's the whole test.

## Making it stick

A format only works if it's predictable. Send it the same day, same time, same subject-line pattern ("[Project] — Friday note, week of Apr 21"). Readers start to expect it, and expectation is what turns a report into a routine instead of an interruption.

Two rules keep it honest:

- **The note is written for the reader, not the writer.** Before you send, read it as someone with fifteen seconds and no context. If the decision isn't obvious, rewrite the decision block.
- **Silence is a signal.** If a week has no movement and no decisions, send three lines saying so. A short honest note builds more trust than a padded one.

If you're rolling this out to a team, don't mandate six blocks on day one. Start with the headline, what moved, and what's stuck. Add the decision block once people trust the format isn't a trap. You can borrow the structure from a [weekly status report template](https://example.com/weekly-status-report-template) if you want something to paste into, but the discipline matters more than the shell.

## Where AI helps — and where it hurts

An AI assistant is genuinely useful for the boring parts: turning your messy notes into clean bullets, tightening the headline, flagging that you wrote "will decide" without a date. It's a copy editor with infinite patience.

It's bad at the part that matters. It doesn't know which blocker is the real one, and it will happily smooth a hard truth into a soft paragraph. Use it to compress, not to decide. Run the draft through a quick human check — does the decision block name a real decision and a real default? — before it goes out. (If you want a repeatable version of that check, a short [AI draft review](https://example.com/ai-draft-review) routine works well here.)

The rule I use: AI drafts the note, a human owns the headline and the decisions. Those two lines are the report.

## The payoff

Teams that switch to a fixed weekly note tend to notice the same things. The Friday status meeting shrinks or disappears because the questions get answered in writing. Escalations arrive earlier, because the "stuck" block makes it normal to name a problem before it's a crisis. And the PM stops rewriting the same update for three different audiences.

None of that comes from the template. It comes from sending the same shape, at the same time, written for the person reading it. Pick your day, write the headline first, and let the rest follow.
