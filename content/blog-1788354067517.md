The energy in the room was electric. The whiteboard was a beautiful chaos of pink, blue, and yellow sticky notes. Your designer sketched a wireframe on the fly. An engineer shouted, “Oh, what if we used the new API for that?” Everyone left the meeting buzzing. You posted a photo of the whiteboard in Slack with a fire emoji. Success.

Then Monday morning happens.

You type into the team channel, “Hey team, great brainstorm on Friday! Who’s picking up the first action item for the ‘Magic Onboarding’ idea?”

Silence.

A lone thumbs-up emoji appears from a designer who is in another meeting. The fire from Friday has been reduced to cold ash. The momentum is gone. The whiteboard full of promises is now just a photo of colorful paper. The brainstorm was an activity, not an outcome.

This isn’t a sign of a lazy team. It’s a sign of a failed facilitation. You confused enthusiasm for commitment.

### You Mistook Brainstorming for Planning

The goal of a brainstorm feels like idea generation. We are taught to diverge, to think big, to say “yes, and…” But we often forget the second, harder part: converging and committing. When you end a meeting on the high of pure ideation, you haven't finished the job. You’ve just completed the fun part.

Here’s where the process breaks down:

*   **Vague "Next Steps":** An idea without a specific, single-threaded owner is just a wish. "We should explore this" is a common phrase that means "no one will explore this."
*   **Enthusiasm as Ownership:** A teammate saying "That's a brilliant idea!" is not the same as them saying "I will spend eight hours of my week figuring out if that brilliant idea is technically feasible." You heard the first part and assumed the second.
