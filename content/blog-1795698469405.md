Most status reports fail the same way: they describe everything and flag nothing. Forty lines of green checkmarks, a paragraph about a vendor call, and buried in line 31, a sentence that starts with "minor concern." The stakeholder reading it on their phone between meetings misses the only thing that mattered.

An exception report flips the format. You report the norm in one line and spend the rest of the page on what's off track. It's the difference between a weather forecast that says "sunny" and one that says "sunny, except a storm cell moving in from the west at 4pm." Same information channel, wildly different usefulness.

Here's how to write one that your leadership actually reads and acts on.

## What an exception report is (and isn't)

An exception report documents only the deviations from plan: the missed milestone, the budget line trending 12% over, the dependency that slipped, the risk that just became real. Everything on-plan gets a single summary line or a dashboard reference.

It is not a status report with the boring parts deleted. The discipline is different. A status report answers "where are we?" An exception report answers "what needs a decision or attention, and what happens if nobody acts?"

That framing matters because it changes who the document is for. Status reports serve everyone. Exception reports serve the people who can unblock you. If your reader can't do anything about the exception, it probably belongs in a different channel.

## The structure that works

I've landed on a five-part format after watching too many exception reports turn into complaint logs. Keep it to one page unless the exception count genuinely warrants more.

**1. Scope line.** One sentence: what this report covers, the reporting period, and the plan baseline you're comparing against. "Covering the checkout redesign, weeks of May 6–17, against the baseline approved April 1." Without a baseline, "exception" is meaningless — you can't be off track from nothing.

**2. On-plan summary.** Two or three lines maximum. Which workstreams are tracking, what shipped, what's next and on schedule. This exists so the reader trusts you're not hiding good news or padding the doc.

**3. Exceptions, ranked.** The core of the report. Each exception gets a consistent block:

- **What deviated** — the fact, with numbers. "Auth integration slipped 6 working days."
- **Why** — one or two sentences, no blame, no hedging. "Vendor's sandbox went down for 48 hours; their fix is deployed and verified."
- **Impact** — what this does to scope, date, cost, or risk. "Launch date holds if we cut SSO from v1. Otherwise we move to June 3."
- **Ask** — the specific decision or help you need, and from whom. "Need your call on the SSO cut by Thursday."

Rank by consequence, not by when you found out. The thing that kills the launch goes first even if it's less emotionally urgent than the thing that annoyed you yesterday.

**4. Watch list.** Items trending toward an exception but not there yet. Keep this to three entries. A watch list that grows every cycle means your thresholds are too tight.

**5. Asks recap.** A short bulleted list of every decision or action needed, with owner and due date. This is the part your reader forwards to their boss, so make it stand alone.

## Set thresholds before you need them

The hardest part of exception reporting isn't writing — it's deciding what counts as an exception. Do this in advance, with your sponsor, and write it down.

Useful thresholds look like:

- Any milestone slip of more than 3 working days
- Any budget line more than 10% over forecast
- Any risk that moved from medium to high likelihood
- Any dependency owned by another team that's more than a week late
- Any decision that's been waiting on someone for more than 5 days

Thresholds do two jobs. They stop you from agonizing over whether a two-day slip is worth mentioning, and they give stakeholders a shared definition of "on track" so nobody argues about the color of the RAG status later. If your org already has escalation criteria, borrow them — consistency beats cleverness.

## Write the exception, not the story of the exception

The most common failure I see: PMs narrate the journey. "We reached out to the vendor on Tuesday, then followed up Wednesday, and by Thursday we'd escalated to their account manager, who confirmed..." The reader doesn't need the timeline. They need the state and the ask.

Compare:

> **Weak:** We've been going back and forth with the vendor about the sandbox outage and it's been frustrating, but they say it should be resolved soon, though we're not totally sure of the timeline, so we may need to look at alternatives.

> **Strong:** Auth integration slipped 6 days (sandbox outage, vendor-side). Launch holds if SSO moves to v1.1; otherwise June 3. Need your call on the SSO cut by Thursday.

The strong version is shorter, states the fact, quantifies the impact, and names the decision. It also doesn't editorialize about frustration — your reader can infer the mood from the numbers.

One rule I enforce on my own drafts: if a sentence doesn't change what the reader knows or does, cut it. Vendor call logs, meeting recaps, and "we continue to monitor" belong in the project wiki, not the exception report.

## Where AI helps and where it hurts

I use AI on exception reports in exactly two places, and I've stopped using it everywhere else.

**Drafting the impact math.** Give a model the slip, the dependency chain, and the launch date, and ask it to work out which downstream milestones are affected and by how many days. It's fast at this and catches second-order effects I miss when I'm close to the problem. Verify the arithmetic yourself — models are confidently wrong about dates.

**Tightening the prose.** Paste your exception block and ask for a version under 40 words that keeps the number, the impact, and the ask. It's a decent editor for removing hedge words. It will also try to soften your language ("may potentially impact"), which is the opposite of what an exception report needs. Reject those edits.

Where AI hurts: letting it write the "why" or the "ask." The model doesn't know your organization's politics, who has decision authority, or which stakeholder needs a heads-up before the formal ask lands. Those judgments are the entire value of the report. Keep them yours.

A quick check before you send: run your draft past the question "could a reader who's never seen this project act on it?" If not, the exception isn't specific enough.

## A worked example

Here's a full exception report, trimmed to what actually went out:

> **Checkout redesign — exception report, May 6–17**
> Baseline: plan approved April 1. On plan: cart UI, payment retry logic, QA environment. Shipped: cart persistence.
>
> **1. Auth integration — 6-day slip.** Vendor sandbox outage (48h) plus their fix verification. Impact: launch holds if SSO moves to v1.1; otherwise June 3. **Ask: approve SSO cut by Thu May 23 — Dana.**
>
> **2. Analytics instrumentation — 12% over budget.** Third-party event schema change forced rework. Impact: $8.4k over the $70k line; absorbed if we drop the funnel dashboard from v1. **Ask: confirm funnel dashboard deferral — Marcus.**
>
> **Watch:** search relevance tuning (2 days behind, recoverable); legal review of new consent copy (due May 24, no buffer).
>
> **Asks recap:** SSO cut decision (Dana, May 23). Funnel dashboard deferral (Marcus, May 22).

That's it. Two exceptions, two asks, a watch list, and a scope line. A VP can read it in 90 seconds and reply with two words.

## Make the cadence match the exceptions

Exception reports work best on a fixed rhythm — weekly for most projects, daily during a crisis — so readers know when to look. But the content volume should flex. A quiet week produces a five-line report, and that's a feature, not laziness. If your exception report is always full, either the project is genuinely in trouble or your thresholds are too tight and you're flagging noise.

The one thing I'd push you to change this week: take your last status report and rewrite it as an exception report. Count how many lines survive. Most PMs find that 80% of the original was on-plan summary, and the 20% that mattered was scattered and unranked. Reordering that 20% by consequence, adding a baseline, and attaching an owner and a date to each ask is the whole job. It takes fifteen minutes and it's the difference between a report that gets filed and one that gets a decision.
