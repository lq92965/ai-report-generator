The CIO is on slide 17 of the digital transformation roadmap. The five-year capital expenditure plan is mapped out in a confident Gantt chart, all the way to a glorious, fully-integrated data future. The numbers are big. The timelines are firm. In the back of the room, the new VP of AI is scrolling through a product announcement from a startup that was founded six months ago. The startup’s new API renders the first two years of the CIO’s plan completely, utterly obsolete.

This isn’t the familiar rhythm of technological progress. This isn’t Moore’s Law, a predictable doubling you could set your watch to. That was a current you could navigate. This is a series of flash floods. The leap from one generation of foundation models to the next doesn’t just make things faster or cheaper. It invalidates the entire problem statement. Why spend 18 months and $10 million building a bespoke system for internal document analysis when a new general model, available via API call, can do it better by next quarter?

The result is a paralysis of long-term planning. Every major architectural decision is now a high-stakes gamble on a future that is fundamentally unknowable. Do you commit to a three-year deal with a cloud provider for a specific type of GPU, knowing a new hardware architecture could halve its value overnight? Do you build your entire product’s intelligence on a single proprietary model, chaining your fate to a vendor whose roadmap is a black box? These are no longer just engineering decisions. They are company-defining bets made on a roulette wheel.

The chaos is bleeding out from the engineering department and into every corner of the business. The marketing team’s carefully planned content strategy is upended by generative tools that change the economics of creation. The finance department’s hiring projections are meaningless when entire categories of analytical work can be automated by a single software update. The very concept of a stable, predictable business process is dissolving.
