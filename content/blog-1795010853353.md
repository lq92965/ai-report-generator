You know the drill. Forty-five minutes on Zoom, six people, a decision that took eight minutes to reach and thirty-seven to circle. Then someone says, "Great, let's sync again Thursday to make sure we're aligned."

That Thursday meeting exists because nobody wrote down what happened on Tuesday. Not because the first meeting failed — because the record of it failed.

A meeting recap email is the cheapest lever you have against calendar creep. Done right, it takes six minutes to write and replaces a recurring invite. Done wrong, it's a transcript dump nobody opens.

Here's the version that works.

## Send it within the hour, not by end of day

The recap has a shelf life. Once people move to their next call, their memory of the discussion starts collapsing into whatever conclusion they personally preferred. By 5 p.m., you're not recording a meeting — you're negotiating a memory.

Write the recap while the tab is still open. I aim for 20 minutes after the call ends, before the next thing starts. It doesn't need to be polished. It needs to be fast and accurate.

If you're running the meeting, this is easier: assign someone to capture decisions and owners live, then paste those into the email. If you're not running it, ask at the top of the call who's writing the recap. The person who volunteers is usually the one who cares most about the outcome — which makes them the right person anyway.

## The four-block structure

Skip the "attendees, agenda, discussion points" skeleton. Nobody reads a discussion summary. They read decisions and next steps. Structure the email in four blocks, in this order:

**1. The decision.** One sentence, past tense, specific. "We're delaying the pricing page launch to March 14 to finish the billing integration." Not "we discussed the pricing page timeline."

**2. What changed.** Two to four bullets on what's different from before this meeting. New dates, dropped scope, changed owner. If nothing changed, say so — "no changes to the current plan" is a valid and useful line.

**3. Owners and dates.** A short list: name, task, due date. One owner per item. If two people share an item, pick one to be accountable and cc the other.

**4. What's still open.** The unresolved questions, with a named person responsible for closing each one and a date. This is the block that prevents the follow-up meeting. An open question without an owner becomes next week's agenda item.

That's it. No recap of who said what. No "great discussion today." No summary of the summary.

## Write the decision so it can be quoted

The hardest line to write is the decision line, because it forces you to commit. Most recaps hedge: "We leaned toward option B but want to revisit." That's not a decision — it's a meeting that will happen again.

If the meeting ended without a decision, write that explicitly: "No decision today. Priya will decide by Thursday based on the cost data." That's still a decision — it's a decision about who decides and when. It closes the loop without pretending consensus exists.

When there is a decision, write it so a person who wasn't in the room can act on it. Test it: could a new hire read this line and know what to do Monday morning? If not, rewrite.

## Name the dissent

This is the part most people skip, and it's the part that saves you.

If someone pushed back on the decision — the engineer who thinks the timeline is fantasy, the designer who wanted the other direction — note it in one line. "Ravi flagged that the March 14 date assumes the vendor contract lands by Feb 28; if it slips, we revisit."

Naming dissent does two things. It tells the skeptic they were heard, which drops the temperature. And it creates a tripwire: if the assumption breaks, the group already agreed on what happens next. You've pre-negotiated the re-open instead of scheduling a meeting to negotiate it.

A recap that pretends everyone agreed is a recap that gets relitigated.

## Keep it under 200 words

Length is a feature. The longer the recap, the less of it gets read, and the more room for someone to reply-all with "actually, I think what we said was..." Short recaps get read on phones between meetings, which is exactly when you want the decision landing in someone's head.

If the meeting genuinely produced a lot — a workshop, a planning session — the recap is still short. Link to the artifact (the doc, the board, the spec) and summarize only the decisions. The email is the index, not the archive.

## The subject line does real work

Use the decision in the subject, not the meeting name. "Pricing page moves to March 14" beats "Recap: Pricing sync 2/11." People scan subject lines and skip bodies. If the decision is in the subject, you've communicated even to the people who never open it.

## When to skip the recap entirely

Not every meeting needs one. If the meeting was a status update with no decisions, the recap is noise. If it was a one-on-one, the recap is weird. If it was a working session where the output is a document, the document is the recap.

Write the recap when a decision was made, an owner changed, or a date moved. Otherwise, let it go.

## A template you can paste

> **Subject:** [Decision in five words]
>
> **Decision:** [One sentence, past tense, specific.]
>
> **What changed:**
> - [Change 1]
> - [Change 2]
>
> **Owners:**
> - [Name] — [task] — [date]
> - [Name] — [task] — [date]
>
> **Still open:**
> - [Question] — [owner] — [date to close]
>
> **Dissent noted:** [One line, or omit.]

Send it to attendees plus anyone affected who wasn't there. Reply-to-all is fine here — that's the point. The people who need to know are often the ones who weren't invited.

The measure of a good recap isn't whether people reply "thanks." It's whether the follow-up meeting gets cancelled. When that happens, you'll know the email did its job.
