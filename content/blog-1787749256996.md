You walk out of the kickoff meeting feeling a surge of momentum. The plan is solid. The VP of Engineering nodded. The marketing lead gave a thumbs-up. Everyone is aligned. You update the roadmap, close the pre-read doc, and move on to the next fire.

Then, a week later, it starts.

The lead engineer DMs you: "Just to clarify, for the V1, we're building the full enterprise-grade permissioning system, right?" The designer presents a mockup that solves a problem you explicitly de-scoped. A support ticket gets escalated from a stakeholder who thought the project was about something else entirely.

The head-nod agreement was an illusion. You didn't have alignment. You had a room full of people who heard what they wanted to hear.

## The Gap Between Consensus and Commitment

This isn't about bad intentions. It’s about the difference between agreeing to a concept and committing to a concrete plan. "We'll improve the onboarding flow" is a concept. Everyone can nod at that. But to you, it means "add three tooltips." To your engineer, it means "refactor the entire user authentication service."

When you leave the room with only vague consensus, you’ve given every stakeholder a blank check to fill in with their own assumptions. The sprint planning meeting becomes the place where these hidden disagreements finally surface, often with frustration and accusations of "changing the plan."

You didn't get alignment in the meeting. You just postponed the misalignment.

## How to Get Real Commitment

The fix is to make the implications of a decision so painfully obvious that no one can misunderstand. You need to translate the abstract into the actual work.

### 1. Articulate the Trade-Offs. Loudly.

Every decision is a trade-off. Your job is to make the trade-off the center of the conversation. Don't just present the chosen path; present the paths you're abandoning to make it happen.

Instead of saying: "We're going to prioritize the new dashboard for Q3."

Say this: "We're prioritizing the new dashboard for Q3. To do this, we are explicitly not redesigning the settings page and we are accepting that the mobile view will be non-responsive for the initial launch. This is the trade-off: speed for scope."

Then, be quiet. Let the silence hang. Someone now has to object to the specific sacrifice, not the general direction. This forces a real conversation about priorities.
