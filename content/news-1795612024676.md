The first thing you notice on the dashboard is the pull counter. It ticks up in the thousands per minute, a number that once felt like a health metric and now reads like a meter spinning outside a rental flat. Somewhere in that traffic is a customer, a competitor, an auditor, and a scraper, and the registry cannot tell you which is which. It only knows that the door opened.

For a decade, the container registry was the least glamorous box in the stack. You pushed an image, you pulled it, you forgot it. Then the supply chain attacks arrived, then the compliance questionnaires, then the egress bills. The registry quietly became the place where trust is manufactured and metered, and the companies running one are discovering they are no longer librarians. They are landlords, and every tenant wants a different lease.

## The rent is real now

Docker's move to restrict unauthenticated pulls on Docker Hub in 2023 was the opening shot, but the pattern has spread. Registries that once absorbed anonymous traffic for free have started asking who is knocking. Artifactory and Harbor deployments inside enterprises now sit behind rate limits, token scopes, and image retention policies that silently delete anything older than ninety days. A team that shipped a working build last quarter can find it gone this quarter, not because anyone decided to remove it, but because a garbage collector did.

The stakes are not abstract. When a registry deletes a base image or a cached layer, the rebuild fails, the incident clock starts, and the postmortem blames the pipeline. The registry is never in the room. It is the landlord who changed the locks while you were on holiday and left a note taped to the door.

## Trust is the new rent

The deeper shift is that registries now sell trust, not storage. Signing, provenance attestations, vulnerability scanning, admission control — these are the features that make a registry worth paying for, and they are also the features that make it a gatekeeper. A registry that can refuse to serve an unsigned image is a registry that decides what runs in production. That is a lot of power for a service most teams configured once and never revisited.

The uncomfortable question is who audits the auditor. When the registry is the source of truth for what code exists, and the same registry is the source of truth for whether that code is safe, you have collapsed two jobs into one box. The box is convenient. The box is also a single point of failure with a billing department.

## What to do about it

The practical answer is boring and correct: treat the registry like infrastructure you own, not a service you consume. Mirror what you depend on. Pin digests, not tags. Keep an offline copy of anything you would be unable to rebuild. Run your own retention policy before someone else's runs on you. None of this is novel, which is precisely why it keeps getting skipped.

The strategic answer is harder. If registries are landlords, then the industry needs tenant protections — portability, transparency about deletion, and a way to leave without losing the deposit. Right now the deposit is your entire build history, and the lease renews automatically.

The pull counter keeps ticking. Somewhere a team is about to discover that the image they have been shipping for two years no longer exists, and no one will be able to tell them exactly when it vanished or why. The registry will simply report that the door is closed, and charge them for the attempt.
