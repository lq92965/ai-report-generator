Your AI notetaker joined the sprint review, produced 2,400 words of transcript, and extracted eleven "action items." Three of them contradict each other. Two are things someone said they *might* do. One is a question that got answered four minutes later but the extractor didn't notice. You paste the summary into Slack anyway, because the alternative is reading the transcript yourself, and the meeting ended twenty minutes ago.

This is the most common failure mode of AI in PM work: it converts a messy conversation into a confident-looking artifact that nobody has verified. The transcript is accurate. The summary is plausible. The decisions are wrong.

Here's a workflow that fixes it.

## The transcript is not the record

A transcript captures what was said. A record captures what was agreed. Those are different documents, and treating them as the same thing is how teams end up re-litigating the same decision two weeks later.

Before you touch an AI summary, decide what you actually need from the meeting. Usually it's three things:

- **Decisions made** — with the owner and the date
- **Open questions** — with who's resolving them and by when
- **Changed commitments** — anything that moves a date, scope, or dependency

Everything else — the discussion, the tangents, the context — is supporting material. Useful if a decision gets challenged, irrelevant otherwise.

AI tools are good at the first pass on all three. They are bad at knowing which of the eleven extracted action items are real commitments versus conversational noise. That judgment is yours.

## A three-pass review that takes fifteen minutes

I run this after any meeting where an AI tool produced notes. It's not glamorous, but it catches the errors that embarrass you later.

**Pass 1: Skim for decisions.** Open the AI summary and look only for statements of the form "we will" or "we decided." For each one, ask: did someone with authority actually commit to this, or did the tool infer it from a suggestion? Cross-check against the transcript timestamp the tool usually provides. If you can't find a clear commitment in the source, cut it.

**Pass 2: Check every action item against a name and a date.** An action item without both is a wish. If the AI extracted "follow up with legal on the data retention question," that's not an action — it's a topic. Rewrite it as "Priya to confirm data retention clause with legal by Thursday" or drop it. Half the extracted items usually fail this test.

**Pass 3: Flag contradictions.** AI extractors are bad at resolving conversational back-and-forth. Someone proposes X, someone else counters with Y, the group lands on Z — and the summary lists X, Y, and Z as parallel items. Read the summary as if you were a stakeholder who wasn't in the room. Does it read like a coherent set of agreements, or like three people talking past each other? If the latter, rewrite the section by hand from the transcript.

Fifteen minutes, maybe twenty on a dense meeting. It's the difference between a record your team trusts and a document that creates new confusion.

## What to send, and what to keep

The reviewed summary goes to the meeting attendees within an hour, ideally. Not the transcript. Not the raw AI output. The reviewed version, with a one-line note: "Reviewed against the transcript; corrections welcome."

Keep the transcript in an archive folder, linked from the summary. It's your audit trail if a decision gets contested. Don't send it to stakeholders — nobody reads 2,400 words, and the parts that matter are already in the summary.

If the meeting produced a decision that changes scope, timeline, or budget, that decision also goes into your decision log with the date and the owner. The meeting summary is a snapshot; the decision log is the durable record. Don't confuse them.

## Where AI actually helps

The tool earns its keep in three places, and it's worth being specific about them:

**Speaker attribution.** Modern notetakers get this right most of the time, which saves you the "who said that" reconstruction that used to eat the first ten minutes of writing up notes.

**Timestamped retrieval.** When a decision gets challenged six weeks later, you can search the transcript for the exact exchange. That's a real improvement over memory.

**First-draft structure.** The AI's summary, even when wrong, gives you a scaffold to edit rather than a blank page. Editing is faster than writing from scratch, and the errors are usually visible on a careful read.

What it doesn't do well: distinguish commitment from speculation, resolve contradictions, or know which items matter to your specific stakeholders. Those are the parts of the job that don't automate.

## The rule that keeps this honest

Never forward AI meeting notes without reading them against the transcript. Not because the tool is bad — it's getting better every quarter — but because the failure mode is silent. A wrong summary looks exactly like a right one. The only defense is a human who was in the room, checking the extracted items against what they remember and what the transcript shows.

Fifteen minutes per meeting. It's the cheapest insurance you'll buy this quarter.
