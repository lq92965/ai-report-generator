You hit "share" and feel a wave of productive relief. The strategy doc is done. All ten pages of it. It details the problem space, the competitive landscape, the proposed solution, the technical trade-offs, and the GTM plan. You add your key stakeholders as commenters, type a cheerful "Would love your feedback!" in the notification message, and lean back. You’ve just avoided another meeting.

An hour later, your inbox is a war zone.

The VP of Sales is questioning your TAM calculation in a comment thread with three replies. The lead engineer has highlighted an entire paragraph with the note, "This is much more complex than it sounds." Marketing wants to know why their launch concerns, which they dropped in a side-channel Slack conversation last Tuesday, aren't addressed on page seven. Your carefully crafted document, intended to create clarity, has become a battlefield of disconnected comments, suggestions, and drive-by critiques.

You didn't avoid a meeting. You created a hundred of them, and you're the host of every single one.

### The Asynchronous Trap

We’ve been conditioned to see meetings as the enemy of deep work. We fetishize the asynchronous-first culture. We believe that if we just write everything down with enough detail, perfect alignment will magically emerge.

This is a fantasy.

A long document isn’t a substitute for a conversation; it’s a canvas for opinions. When you send a doc out for "feedback," you aren't asking for alignment. You're inviting people to poke holes, bikeshed on wording, and relitigate settled arguments in the margins. You’ve traded a focused, high-bandwidth discussion for a chaotic, low-context free-for-all.

The problem isn't the document itself. The problem is the job you’re hiring it to do.

### The Right Tool for the Job: A Simple Framework

Stop asking if you can replace a meeting with a doc. Start asking what outcome you actually need.

**Write the Doc For Informing.**
A document is the right tool when the goal is to create a durable record or broadcast information one-way. It’s for settling, not debating.

Use a doc when you need to:
*   **Codify a decision that has already been made.** This is the "single source of truth" for what was decided and why.
*   **Provide a detailed technical specification.** Engineers need a precise reference to build from, not a philosophical treatise.
*   **Onboard a new team member.** A well-structured set of documents provides context and answers common questions.
*   **Share a final project update.** The work is done; this is the record of what was shipped and what the impact was.

**Call the Meeting For Aligning.**
A meeting is the right tool when the goal is to synthesize different viewpoints, navigate complexity, and commit to a path forward together.

Use a meeting when you need to:
*   **Debate high-stakes trade-offs.** Should we sacrifice performance for a faster launch? You can't solve that in a comment thread.
*   **Explore an ambiguous problem.** When you don't even know what the right questions are, you need to brainstorm in real-time.
*   **Gain genuine buy-in from a divided group.** Seeing faces and hearing tone of voice builds the trust necessary for real commitment.
*   **Unblock a team.** When a project is stuck, you need to get the right people in a room to make a decision and move on.

### Your 10-Minute Alignment Playbook

That meeting you’re avoiding doesn’t need to be an hour-long slog. A focused, 10-minute sync can accomplish more than 100 Google Doc comments.

1.  **Set the Stage.** Don't send a pre-read. Send a single sentence in the invite: "We are meeting to decide X. The two options are A or B." This frames the discussion immediately.
2.  **State the Goal Upfront.** The first words out of your mouth should be: "Thanks for joining. The purpose of this meeting is to choose between A and B. We will leave with a decision."
3.  **Present the Options (2 Mins).** Briefly state the case for each option and its primary trade-off. No deep dives.
4.  **Debate (6 Mins).** Open the floor. Keep the conversation focused on the decision at hand. If someone goes on a tangent, gently steer them back: "That's an interesting point for later, but for now, how does it help us choose between A and B?"
