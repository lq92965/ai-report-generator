You typed "write a status update for the Atlas migration" into your AI tool. Eight seconds later you have four paragraphs that sound like a consultant wrote them. You skim, change one date, and hit send.

Two hours later your VP replies: "Wait — is the API work done or not?"

The draft said "significant progress on integration." That's not a status. That's a mood. And you shipped it because it read smoothly, and smooth reads feel true.

Here's the fix I use, and the one I teach PMs on my team: a 20-minute review pass before any AI-drafted message leaves your outbox. Not a rewrite. A check. Five specific things, in order, every time.

## Why AI drafts fail in the same way every time

AI writing tools are trained to produce text that sounds confident and complete. That's the opposite of what a status update needs. A good update says "here is what I know, here is what I don't, here is what I need from you." An AI draft says "the team is making excellent progress across multiple workstreams."

The failure modes cluster. In my experience reviewing hundreds of these drafts, five problems account for almost all the damage:

1. **Vague progress language** — "significant," "ongoing," "on track" with no evidence attached.
2. **Invented specificity** — the model fills gaps with plausible numbers or dates you never gave it.
3. **Buried asks** — the thing you need from the reader ends up in paragraph four.
4. **False balance** — the draft presents risks and non-risks with equal weight, so nothing stands out.
5. **Tone drift** — it's warmer or more formal than you'd ever sound, which signals to readers that a machine wrote it.

None of these get caught by a skim. A skim checks for typos and vibes. You need a checklist that checks for claims.

## The five-pass review, in order

Run these passes in this sequence. Each one takes three to five minutes. Do them in a single sitting if you can — context-switching between drafts is where errors survive.

### Pass 1: The claim audit

Read the draft with one question per sentence: *Could I defend this in a meeting if someone pushed back?*

Highlight every sentence that makes a claim about progress, timeline, or risk. For each one, ask where the evidence is. If you can't point to a ticket, a Slack thread, or a meeting where someone said it out loud, cut the sentence or soften it to what you actually know.

"Significant progress on integration" becomes "Auth service is done and deployed to staging; payment webhook work is 60% complete and blocked on the vendor's sandbox access."

That's longer. It's also the difference between a reader who trusts you and one who schedules a follow-up call to find out what's real.

### Pass 2: The number hunt

AI models are weirdly eager to produce numbers. Dates, percentages, headcounts, dollar figures — they appear in drafts you never fed a single statistic to.

Scan for every digit in the draft. For each one, confirm you supplied it. If you didn't, delete it or replace it with the actual figure from your source of truth. A wrong date in a status update costs more trust than a missing one, because the reader assumes you checked.

I keep a simple rule: no number goes out that I didn't type myself or pull from a system I can name.

### Pass 3: The ask extraction

Find the sentence that tells the reader what you need from them. If it's not in the first three lines, move it up. If it doesn't exist, write it.

Most AI drafts bury the ask because the training data is full of corporate prose that softens requests into suggestions. "It might be worth considering whether the team could potentially..." is not an ask. "I need your sign-off on the vendor contract by Thursday" is an ask.

Rewrite every request as a direct sentence with a named owner and a date. If you can't name the owner, the ask isn't ready to send.

### Pass 4: The risk sort

AI drafts tend to list risks in a flat, even tone, as if a blocked dependency and a minor staffing question carry the same weight. Readers can't tell what matters, so they treat everything as urgent or nothing as urgent. Both are bad.

Sort the risks yourself. Mark the one or two that could actually derail the project this month. Cut or demote the rest. A status update with one clear risk and a proposed mitigation reads as competent. One with seven risks reads as a cry for help.

### Pass 5: The voice check

Read the draft out loud. Anything you wouldn't say to a colleague in a hallway gets rewritten.

This pass catches the small stuff that signals "a robot wrote this": over-formal transitions, three-item lists where two would do, the word "moreover." It also catches the bigger problem — a draft that sounds like a different person than the one who's been running the project for six months. Stakeholders notice. They start discounting your updates because they can't tell which parts are yours.

## What to keep from the AI draft

After five passes you might have rewritten half the text. That's fine. The draft still saved you time in three places:

- **Structure.** The model gave you a skeleton — opening, progress, risks, asks — that you'd otherwise spend ten minutes assembling.
- **Coverage.** It reminded you to mention the thing you'd forgotten, like the vendor renewal or the hiring update.
- **First-draft momentum.** Starting from something is easier than starting from a blank page, even when the something needs work.

The mistake isn't using AI to draft. It's treating the draft as finished because it reads well. Fluency is not accuracy. Your review pass is what converts one into the other.

## A quick before/after

Here's a real pair from a project I ran last quarter.

**AI draft, sent as-is:**
> The Atlas migration continues to make strong progress. The team has completed several key milestones and remains on track for the Q3 delivery. There are some minor risks around vendor coordination, but mitigation plans are in place. We'll continue to monitor and provide updates as needed.

**After the five passes:**
> Atlas migration status: auth service live in staging as of Monday. Payment webhooks are 60% done and blocked — vendor sandbox access is still pending their ticket #4471, open since the 12th.
>
> Risk: if sandbox access doesn't land by Friday, the Q3 date slips two weeks. I've escalated to their account manager; no response yet.
>
> Ask: can you ping your contact at the vendor today? A nudge from you will move faster than mine.

The second one is longer. It's also the version that got a reply in nine minutes and unblocked the vendor that afternoon.

## Make it a habit, not a heroic effort

The 20-minute review only works if it's automatic. I block 4:00 to 4:20 on days I send stakeholder updates and treat it like any other meeting. The checklist lives in a note pinned to my toolbar. Five passes, in order, no skipping.

After a month you'll internalize the passes and the review drops to ten minutes. After three months you'll start writing the prompt differently — feeding the AI the specific claims, numbers, and asks you want included, so the draft arrives closer to finished and the review gets faster still.

That's the real payoff. The review pass doesn't just catch bad drafts. It trains you to brief the tool better next time, which is the skill that actually compounds.
