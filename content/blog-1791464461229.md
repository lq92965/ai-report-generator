It’s 3 PM on a Tuesday. A new engineer on your team posts in the main Slack channel: “Hey, quick question—why are we using a third-party service for notifications instead of our own message queue? Seems like it would be cheaper to build.”

You feel a familiar twitch. You typed out a detailed explanation for this exact question three weeks ago for a director in marketing. Six weeks ago, you walked a new designer through the same logic. The original decision doc is buried somewhere in a forgotten Google Drive folder, and the key conversation happened in a Slack thread that has long since been archived.

So you spend the next 15 minutes re-typing the context, the options you considered, and the trade-offs you made. Fifteen minutes you could have spent on the next problem.

This isn’t just an annoyance. This is a silent killer of team velocity. When the *why* behind a decision isn't captured and easily accessible, you’re doomed to re-litigate it forever. Onboarding slows to a crawl. Good decisions get overturned by people with incomplete context. Institutional knowledge walks out the door every time a team member leaves.

You don’t need more meetings or longer documents. You need a decision log.

### More Signal, Less Noise

A decision log is not meeting minutes. It’s not a project plan. It’s a simple, running document that serves as the single source of truth for significant choices made by your team. It’s asynchronous, searchable, and brutally efficient.

Its purpose is to answer "Why did we do it this way?" so you never have to.

Creating one isn't bureaucratic overhead; it's an investment in future clarity. The five minutes it takes to document a decision today saves hours of confusion and debate next quarter.

### The Anatomy of a Useful Decision Log Entry

Keep it simple. A bloated template will never get used. Each entry in your log should capture the essentials in a few clear bullet points.

**1. Decision:** State the outcome in a single, declarative sentence. No ambiguity.
*   *Example:* We are moving our user authentication from our legacy internal service to Auth0.

**2. Context & Problem:** What triggered this? What problem were you trying to solve? A few sentences are all you need.
*   *Example:* Our current login system suffers from frequent outages (3 hours of downtime last month) and doesn't support SAML for enterprise customers, which is blocking three major deals.

**3. Options Considered:** Briefly list the 2-3 other viable paths you explored. This shows you did your homework.
*   *Example:*
    *   A) Rebuild our internal service from scratch.
    *   B) Integrate with Okta.
    *   C) Integrate with Auth0.

**4. Rationale & Trade-offs:** This is the most critical section. Why did you pick the winning option? Just as important, what did you willingly sacrifice?
*   *Example:* We chose Auth0 for its superior developer documentation and faster implementation time (est. 2 sprints vs. 6+ for a rebuild). We are accepting a higher per-user cost in exchange for speed to market and offloading maintenance to a dedicated provider. Okta was more expensive and less flexible for our specific use case.

**5. Key People:** Tag the decision-maker and any key contributors or consultants.
*   *Example:* Decision-maker: Sarah (PM). Consulted: Lee (Eng Lead), Maria (Security).

That’s it. Five small sections. The entire entry can be written in the time it takes to compose a single thoughtful Slack message.

### Make It a Destination, Not an Archive

The best decision log is useless if nobody can find it.

Don't just create another loose doc in the void. Pin it to the top of your team's Slack channel. Link it prominently in your Confluence or Notion space. Make it part of your team's onboarding checklist.

When someone asks "why," your first instinct shouldn't be to start typing. It should be to grab the link to the decision log entry. This trains the entire organization to look there first. It respects everyone's time and reinforces the value of clear, documented thinking.

Stop answering the same questions. Write the answer down once, then get back to building what's next.
