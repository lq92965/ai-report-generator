It starts with a single line in a "Getting Started" guide. You’re a developer tasked with adding a new service—analytics, payments, feature flags, whatever. The API documentation is clean, but it gently steers you away from raw HTTP requests. Instead, it points to a friendly command: `npm install the_vendor`.

So easy. You add the package, import the library, and initialize it with your API key. In twenty minutes, you have a working integration. The SDK handles authentication. It manages retries and network hiccups. It gives you beautifully structured objects instead of messy JSON blobs you’d have to parse yourself. Your pull request is a dozen lines long. You’ve saved the team two weeks of tedious work. Everyone is happy.

This is the promise of the Software Development Kit. It’s a box of pre-fabricated solutions, a productivity multiplier.

Six months later, the vendor sends an email announcing a "realignment" of their pricing tiers. Your company’s usage now costs four times as much. The CFO asks for an estimate to migrate to a cheaper competitor. You open the codebase and a cold wave washes over you.

The vendor's `User` object is everywhere. Your application’s internal logic is now deeply entangled with the specific methods and error classes thrown by the SDK. Functions that have nothing to do with the vendor are now passing its proprietary objects around. Your code doesn't just *talk to* the service; it *thinks* like the service. You didn't just build a bridge to a third party. You let them design your city's road network.

Swapping them out is no longer a matter of changing a base URL and an auth header. It’s a fundamental rewrite. A multi-month project that requires touching hundreds of files and halting all other work. The company is cornered. It will pay the new price.

This is the silent bargain of the modern SDK. It offers convenience today in exchange for control tomorrow. It is the most effective vector for vendor lock-in, operating at a level far more insidious than a data silo or a proprietary file format. It colonizes your application's architecture. It rewrites your team's mental models to fit its own.

When you `import vendor`, you are making a commitment. You are pledging allegiance to their data structures, their design choices, and their business model. That single line of code is not just an implementation detail. It’s a loyalty oath, and breaking it will cost you.
