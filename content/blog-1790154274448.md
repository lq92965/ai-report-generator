It’s 15 minutes into your meeting. You’re on slide 8 of 25. You’re sharing your screen, narrating bullet points about the Q3 strategy. You pause for a breath and ask, "Any questions so far?"

Silence. A few cursors dance around the screen. Someone unmutes to say, "Nope, this is great, thanks," and then mutes again. You click to the next slide. You are broadcasting. You are transferring information. But you are wasting the most expensive resource in the company: an hour of synchronous, focused time from ten different people.

This isn't a meeting. It's a monologue that everyone was forced to attend.

### The Myth of the "Informational" Meeting

We book these "read-outs" or "share-outs" with good intentions. We want to get everyone on the same page. We want to provide context. So we assemble a group, pull up our meticulously crafted deck, and talk *at* them for 45 minutes, saving a few minutes at the end for questions that never come.

Here’s the problem: Information transfer is an asynchronous activity. Decision-making is a synchronous sport. Your read-out mashes them together and fails at both.

The audience passively receives information they could have read in 10 minutes on their own time. You, the presenter, get polite nods instead of the critical feedback you desperately need to make the plan better. The meeting ends, a calendar event turns gray, and nothing has actually changed.

### The Asynchronous Hand-Off

Your new default shouldn’t be to schedule a meeting. It should be to send a pre-read.

This isn’t just about attaching a document to the calendar invite. It’s a formal hand-off. It requires a clear contract with your audience.

1.  **Send it early.** At least 24 hours in advance. 48 is better.
2.  **Be specific about the required action.** Don't just say "FYI." State the expectation clearly in the email or Slack message: "Please review this 4-page doc before our meeting on Thursday. Come prepared to discuss the three proposed options on page 3."
3.  **Prime them with questions.** Guide their thinking. Ask them to consider: "What is the biggest unaddressed risk in this plan?" or "Which customer segment will this impact most?" This frames the document as a starting point for a conversation, not a final declaration.

By front-loading the information transfer, you free up the meeting itself for higher-value work.

### Your Meeting Is Now a Workshop

When you walk into the room (or join the video call), assume everyone has done the reading. Do not waste the first 20 minutes recapping the document. That just trains people that the pre-read is optional.

Instead, your new agenda looks like this:

*   **(5 min) Clarifying Questions:** "What parts of the doc were unclear?" Answer directly. Resist the urge to present.
*   **(20 min) Debate the Core Tension:** Frame the main event around a specific, difficult question. "Let's focus on Option A vs. Option B. Marketing, what’s your take on the go-to-market risk for A? Engineering, what’s the hidden complexity in B?" This is where the real work happens. Use a whiteboard. Unpack assumptions.
*   **(15 min) Decide and Commit:** The goal is not consensus; it's clarity. Force a decision on the core issue. If you can't, the decision is to assign a single owner to go gather the missing information. Document the decision, the rationale, and the next steps in real-time for everyone to see.

This structure transforms a passive audience into active participants. You’re no longer a narrator; you’re a facilitator.

The goal of a meeting isn't to "share." It's to decide. Stop scheduling read-outs that could have been a memo. Send the memo, then use that precious time together to argue, align, and build something better than you could have on your own.
