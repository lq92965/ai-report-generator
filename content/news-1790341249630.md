The Zoom window felt like a courtroom dock. Anya watched her manager share his screen, pulling up the timeline of the outage. The document was titled "SEV-1 Postmortem," but everyone in the virtual room knew its real name: The Search for a Scapegoat. The air was thick with the practiced language of blamelessness. “Contributing factors.” “Detection opportunities.” “Action items.” Each phrase was a carefully polished stone, smooth and cold, designed to build a wall around the real story.

The blameless postmortem was once a sacred rite. Borrowed from aviation safety, its purpose was to create psychological safety. It was a promise that if you told the truth about how the system *actually* worked—the kludges, the undocumented dependencies, the tribal knowledge—you wouldn’t be punished. This was the only way to find the latent, systemic failures that lurked beneath the surface of a single human error. The goal wasn’t to find a person to blame; it was to find a process to fix.

That promise has been broken. In today’s world of impossible deadlines and brittle, interconnected services, the postmortem has been weaponized. It has become a piece of corporate theater. The narrative is written before the meeting even starts. The root cause is no longer a discovery to be made, but a verdict to be delivered. It is far easier to sacrifice a junior engineer who pushed a bad config than it is to admit the entire deployment system is a house of cards built on years of neglected technical debt. It is cleaner to point to a single faulty API call than to confront the fact that the org chart creates perverse incentives and communication black holes.

So the trial begins. The on-call engineer is cross-examined. Timestamps are scrutinized. Slack messages are entered into evidence. The "Five Whys" exercise, meant to drill down to a systemic cause, stops conveniently at a single person's mistake. The resulting "action items" are a joke. They are small, cosmetic fixes: "Add more alerting," "Update the runbook," "Improve validation logic." These tasks treat the symptom, not the disease. They do nothing to address the understaffing, the unrealistic roadmap, or the culture of fear that led to the error in the first place.

The real sentence isn't handed down to the engineer who gets a quiet talking-to or is shuffled to a less critical project. The real sentence is passed on the organization itself. When postmortems become show trials, engineers learn to stop telling the truth. They become defensive. They hide mistakes. They write code not to be efficient or correct, but to be legally defensible in the next outage hearing. The system doesn't get more resilient; it gets more opaque. The institutional knowledge required to run the damn thing evaporates as the best people leave for companies that still believe in learning.

The document gets filed away in a Confluence page, a tidy little story of a problem identified and solved. But the real failure—the one that guarantees the next outage is just around the corner—was never even on the agenda.
