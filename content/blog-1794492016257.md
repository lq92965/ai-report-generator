The update went out at 9:02 on a Tuesday. By 9:15, three people had replied with the same question: "So are we on track or not?"

You wrote six paragraphs. You mentioned the migration, the vendor delay, the two bugs, the design review, the hiring loop, and the fact that the team is "cautiously optimistic." You never actually said whether the launch is happening on the 14th.

That's the problem with most progress updates. They report activity. They don't answer the question everyone is holding.

Here's how to fix it, using a structure you can write in about ten minutes.

## Lead With the Answer, Not the Journey

Open with a single sentence that states the current status in plain terms. Not "we've made good progress on the migration" — that's a mood, not a status.

Write this instead: "Launch stays on the 14th. One open risk: the vendor API change may slip two days, which we can absorb."

Two sentences. The reader knows where they stand, what's threatening it, and whether they need to do anything. Everything after that is supporting detail they can skim or skip.

If you're unsure whether you're on track, say so explicitly: "Launch date at risk. We'll know by Thursday whether the vendor fix lands in time." Ambiguity dressed up as optimism is what triggers the 9:15 replies.

## The Four-Part Structure That Covers Everything

Every progress update answers four questions. Keep them in this order.

**1. Status line.** One or two sentences. On track, at risk, or blocked. Include the date or milestone you're measuring against.

**2. What changed since last update.** Only the deltas. Not a recap of the whole project. If nothing changed, write "No change from last week" and move on — that's a valid and useful update.

**3. Risks and decisions needed.** Name the risk, name the owner, name the date you need an answer by. "Legal review of the data retention clause is the blocker. Priya needs sign-off from counsel by Wednesday or the launch slips a week."

**4. Next checkpoint.** When they'll hear from you again and what will be different. "Next update Thursday after the vendor call."

That's it. Four sections, under 200 words for most projects. If your update runs past a screen, you're either managing a genuinely complex program or you're hiding the lead in paragraph five.

## Write for the Reader Who Skims

Most people reading your update are doing it between meetings on their phone. They will read the first line, maybe the bolded bits, and nothing else.

So bold the things that matter. Put the status in the subject line of the email or the first line of the Slack post. If the launch date moved, that fact goes in the subject: "Launch moved to the 21st — vendor API delay."

A useful test: cover everything except the first line and the bolded text. Can a reader still make a decision? If not, your formatting is doing the wrong work.

## What to Leave Out

Progress updates attract filler. Here's what to cut:

- **Meeting recaps.** "We had a productive sync with design" tells the reader nothing. Either a decision came out of it or it doesn't belong.
- **Percentage complete.** "70% done" is a number that means nothing to anyone outside the team and invites arguments about whether it's really 70 or 65. Report milestones hit and milestones remaining.
- **Hedging language.** "Should be fine," "hopefully," "fingers crossed." Either you have a plan or you have a risk. Say which.
- **Kudos paragraphs.** Genuine recognition belongs in a team channel, not buried in a status update where it dilutes the signal.

## A Filled-In Example

Here's what it looks like in practice, for a checkout redesign:

> **Status:** On track for the March 14 launch. Two of three workstreams complete.
>
> **Since last update:** Payment provider integration shipped to staging. QA found four issues, all minor, two already fixed.
>
> **Risk:** The fraud-check vendor hasn't confirmed the new API contract. If we don't have written confirmation by Wednesday, we delay launch one week. Owner: Marcus. Decision needed by Wednesday EOD.
>
> **Next checkpoint:** Thursday standup notes, plus a go/no-go call Friday at 10.

That's 85 words. It answers the question, names the risk, assigns an owner, and sets the next checkpoint. No one replies asking "so are we on track?"

## The Habit That Makes It Stick

Write the update before you check your notes, not after. Start with the status line and the risk, then fill in the supporting detail. If you draft the activity recap first, you'll spend twenty minutes summarizing meetings that don't matter and bury the one fact your stakeholders need.

Set a recurring 15-minute block on your calendar for the day before your update is due. Draft it, cut a third of the words, send it. The discipline of writing the status first forces you to actually know it — and if you don't, that's the most valuable thing the exercise will tell you all week.
