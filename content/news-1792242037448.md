The lawyer stood before the judge, explaining that the chatbot made him do it. He had used the new AI research tool to prepare a brief, and it had, with unnerving confidence, cited six completely fabricated judicial decisions. The cases looked real. They sounded real. They just didn't exist. When pressed, the lawyer’s defense was simple: he was unaware that the AI could be wrong. He didn't know it could lie.

Tech companies have a gentle, almost clinical term for this phenomenon: "hallucination." The word suggests a fleeting, unpredictable break from reality, an aberration in an otherwise sound mind. It’s a clever bit of framing. It anthropomorphizes the machine while simultaneously absolving its creators of responsibility. The model just had a funny turn. It’s a known, quirky side effect we’re all working hard to fix.

This is a dangerous fiction. A large language model is not a mind that can be detached from reality. It is a prediction engine. Its entire function is to calculate the most statistically probable next word, and the word after that, stringing them together into sentences that seem coherent. It has no concept of truth, only of plausibility. When it generates a fake legal precedent or a nonexistent medical study, it isn't hallucinating. It is operating exactly as designed.

The term “hallucination” is a strategic masterpiece of misdirection. It moves the error from the boardroom to the black box. It creates a new, non-human entity to take the fall. Consider Air Canada, which tried to deny a refund promised to a customer by its own support chatbot. The airline argued in court that it could not be held liable for information provided by the bot, portraying it as a separate entity with its own confused thoughts. The tribunal, thankfully, didn't buy it.

But for every case that clear, a thousand others live in the gray area created by this language. When a model offers dangerously wrong advice, who is at fault? The company that trained it on a universe of unvetted data? The engineers who fine-tuned its behavior? Or the algorithm that simply had a “hallucination”? The term itself is a pre-built legal defense. It’s a get-out-of-jail-free card for shipping a product that is fundamentally unreliable.

We are building a world on top of these systems. They are writing code, drafting contracts, and providing medical summaries. Allowing their inevitable, systemic failures to be written off as quirky “hallucinations” is an act of profound corporate negligence. It’s like an automaker blaming a faulty brake line on the car’s “spontaneous kinetic disagreement.”

It’s not a hallucination. It’s a defect. It’s not an anomaly. It's a feature. We must stop using the sanitized language of the lab to describe the real-world harms of these systems. A machine cannot be a scapegoat. The fault lies not in our silicon stars, but in ourselves.
