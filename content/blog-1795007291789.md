Last Tuesday I watched a PM named Dana paste the same 400-word context paragraph into ChatGPT for the fourth time that week. She was drafting a release note, then a stakeholder email, then a help center article, then a Slack announcement. Each time she retyped the product background, the audience, the tone rules, the banned phrases. Each time she got a decent result. Each time she threw the prompt away.

Six weeks later, a colleague asked her how she got such clean release notes so fast. Dana opened her notes app and found nothing. The prompts were gone. The knowledge lived in her fingers and nowhere else.

That is the quiet failure mode of AI adoption on product teams. Not hallucination. Not security. Amnesia. People get real value from these tools and then lose the recipe, so every session starts from zero and every good output is a lucky accident.

## The problem with "I just use AI for stuff"

Most PM teams I talk to have no idea what their AI usage actually looks like. Ask five PMs how they use it and you get five vague answers: "for drafting," "for summarizing," "for brainstorming." That tells you nothing you can act on.

Here is a better question: **which prompts produced output you shipped without rewriting?**

That question forces specificity. It surfaces the three or four prompts per person that genuinely earn their keep, and it exposes the twenty that were a waste of an afternoon. Without that inventory, you cannot onboard new team members, you cannot spot where the tool is quietly making things worse, and you cannot tell whether the $20/month per seat is buying leverage or just novelty.

## The prompt log: one row per keeper

A prompt log is a shared spreadsheet with five columns. That is the whole system.

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Prompt name | Short label, e.g. "Release note from ticket" |
| Prompt text | The exact text, including variables in `[brackets]` |
| When to use it | One sentence on the trigger |
| Output check | What a human must verify before using the result |
| Owner | Who maintains it |

The fifth column matters more than it looks. A prompt with no owner rots. Someone changes the model, the output quality shifts, and nobody notices until a release note goes out with the wrong tense and a customer tweets about it.

### What earns a spot

Not every prompt belongs in the log. Apply a simple bar: the prompt has to have produced output you used at least twice without a full rewrite. That is it. If you had to rewrite it, it was a draft, not a tool.

I keep a separate "graveyard" tab for prompts that looked promising and failed. It sounds like overhead. It is the most-read tab on the sheet, because it stops the next person from spending an hour rediscovering that the model cannot reliably infer priorities from a Jira export.

## A worked example

Here is a real entry from a log I helped a B2B team build. It replaced the single most-repeated task on their team: turning a shipped ticket into a customer-facing release note.

**Prompt name:** Release note from ticket

**Prompt text:**
```
You are writing a release note for [product name], a [one-line description].
Audience: [admin users / end users / both].
Ticket details: [paste ticket title and description].
Write 3 sentences. Sentence 1: what changed. Sentence 2: who it affects.
Sentence 3: what they need to do, or "No action needed."
Do not use the words "seamless," "robust," or "leverage."
Do not invent features not in the ticket.
```

**When to use:** Any ticket tagged `customer-facing` when it moves to Done.

**Output check:** Confirm the "who it affects" line matches the actual rollout scope. The model guesses wrong about this roughly one time in five.

**Owner:** Dana.

Notice what the log does that a chat history does not. It captures the *check* — the specific thing that goes wrong one time in five. That single line is worth more than the prompt itself, because it tells a new PM exactly where to slow down.

## Why this beats a prompt library

There are a hundred "1,000 best AI prompts for product managers" collections online. They are useless for your team, because they were written for nobody's product in particular.

Your log is different in three ways. It is scoped to your product and your vocabulary. It carries verification instructions, which generic libraries never do. And it has an owner, so it stays current when the underlying model changes — which it will, without warning, roughly every few months.

The verification column is not a formality. Every prompt in your log should have a known failure mode written next to it. If you cannot name what goes wrong, you have not used the prompt enough times to trust it.

## The 30-minute ritual that keeps it alive

A log nobody updates is a dead document by week three. The fix is a short recurring habit, not a big quarterly cleanup.

Once a week, in your existing team sync, spend ten minutes on three questions:

1. Did anyone ship something this week that started as an AI draft? Add the prompt if it is a keeper.
2. Did any logged prompt produce bad output? Update the output-check line.
3. Is any prompt now redundant because the model handles it natively? Move it to the graveyard.

Ten minutes. The reason this works is that it rides on a meeting people already attend, and it asks for something concrete — a shipped output — rather than an abstract "share your AI tips."

Rotate who runs it. If the same person always does, the log becomes that person's project and dies when they go on vacation.

## What to keep out of the log

Two categories of prompt should never be logged, and I have watched teams learn this the hard way.

**Anything with customer data in the prompt text.** Log the template with `[paste ticket]` placeholders, never a filled-in example. A prompt log is a shared document; treat it like you would treat a public wiki.

**Anything that makes a judgment call you would not delegate.** Prompts that rank features, score opportunities, or draft performance feedback do not belong in a shared library, because the log implies "this is safe to run without thinking." Some things should require thinking every single time.

The log is for repeatable, checkable, low-stakes drafting. Everything else stays in your head, where it belongs.

## Start with three

You do not need a taxonomy or a governance framework. Open a spreadsheet, add the five columns, and fill in the three prompts you used most this month. Write down the check for each one. Assign yourself as owner.

Then put "prompt log — 10 min" on next week's agenda and see whether anyone adds a row. If they do, you have a system. If they do not, you have a spreadsheet, and you will know within a month which one you actually built.
