The best compliment a weekly report can get is silence from your manager and a forward to their boss. That forward is the whole game. It means your update was clear enough to travel without you standing next to it explaining what you meant.

Most weekly reports die in the inbox because they answer the wrong question. They tell the reader what you did. What your manager needs is what changed, what's at risk, and what decision is coming. Those are three different things, and they belong in three different places on the page.

Here's a structure that survives the forward.

## Start with the one thing that moved

Open with a single sentence naming the most consequential change since last week. Not the busiest thing. The most consequential one.

Bad: "Worked on the onboarding flow and had several syncs with design."

Better: "Onboarding completion is up from 41% to 53% after we cut the address step."

The second version gives your manager something to say in their own staff meeting. The first gives them nothing to repeat.

If nothing consequential changed, say so plainly. "Quiet week on delivery; the real story is the vendor contract slipping two weeks." A quiet week honestly reported beats a busy week dressed up.

## Separate status from signal

Most reports blur two questions: *How is the project tracking?* and *What does the reader need to know or do?* Those deserve different sections.

Status is the boring, predictable part. Green, yellow, red, with a sentence of why. Keep it to three lines per workstream, max. If a reader wants detail, they'll ask.

Signal is where you earn your keep. This is the part where you flag the thing that will bite in three weeks if nobody acts now. Write it as a claim, not a hedge:

- "The payments migration will miss the March 15 date unless we get a second backend engineer by Feb 20."
- "Two enterprise customers have asked for SSO in the last week; that's now the top blocker on renewal conversations."

Notice both of these name a date and a specific consequence. Vague risk language ("there may be some timeline pressure") is the fastest way to get your report skimmed and forgotten.

## Put the ask in its own block

If you need a decision, a resource, or an introduction, give it a labeled section and a deadline. One ask per report is ideal. Two is fine. Five means you haven't prioritized.

The format that works:

> **Decision needed by Thursday:** Do we ship the pricing change to 10% of users or wait for the billing fix? Recommendation: ship to 5% and hold the rest. If I don't hear back by Thursday EOD, I'll go with the 5% plan.

The last sentence matters more than it looks. It converts a question into a default. Managers are busy; a default with a deadline gets a response. An open question floats.

## Keep a running "decided" log

Add a short section at the bottom listing decisions made since the last report, with dates. This does two things. It creates an audit trail you'll want at review time. And it quietly shows your manager that you're closing loops, not just opening them.

Three or four bullets. No more.

## Write for the reader who wasn't in the room

Your report will get forwarded to someone who has no context. That person doesn't know your project codenames, your internal acronyms, or why the billing fix matters. Write for them.

The test: hand your draft to a colleague on a different team. If they can't tell you what changed and what's at risk in under a minute, rewrite it.

This is also where AI tools help, with a caveat. A drafting assistant is good at tightening sentences and catching jargon. It is bad at knowing which change actually mattered. Do the judgment yourself, then let the tool do the polish. If you hand the whole thing to a model, you'll get a report that reads fine and says nothing.

## A template you can copy

```
# Weekly Report — [Name] — Week of [Date]

**Headline:** [One sentence: the most consequential change]

## Status
- [Workstream]: [G / Y / R] — [one line why]
- [Workstream]: [G / Y / R] — [one line why]

## Signal
- [Risk or opportunity, with a date and a consequence]
- [Second item if needed]

## Decision needed
- [Question] by [date]. Recommendation: [your call]. Default if no response: [what you'll do].

## Decided this week
- [Decision] — [date]
- [Decision] — [date]

## Next week
- [Two or three things, no more]
```

Fill it in on Friday afternoon in fifteen minutes. If it takes longer, you're writing too much, not thinking too little.

## The habit that makes it stick

The report is only as good as the week that fed it. Keep a scratch file open all week and drop in one line whenever something changes — a decision, a risk, a number that moved. By Friday you're assembling, not remembering. The people whose reports get forwarded are almost never the ones who write best on the fly. They're the ones who captured the raw material when it happened.

Start with the headline. If you can't write one honest sentence about what changed this week, that's the real signal, and it's worth telling your manager before Friday.
