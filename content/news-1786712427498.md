# The Bug Report Is Now a Philosophical Question

Somewhere between filing the ticket and assigning it a priority, the modern bug report stopped describing a defect and started describing a disagreement about what software is. This is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a logistics problem that product teams are now paying for in sprint velocity and legal exposure.

The shift happened quietly. For most of computing history, a bug was a deviation from a specification. The spec said the button submits the form. The button does not submit the form. A human wrote both statements, a human can adjudicate the gap, and the fix is either a code change or a spec change. The report was a factual claim about a mismatch.

That model assumed a human authored the behavior. Increasingly, that assumption is false.

## When the Spec Is a Probability Distribution

A large language model does not implement a specification. It implements a distribution over plausible continuations, weighted by training data and sampling parameters. When a user reports that the assistant "lied" or "refused" or "hallucinated a citation," there is no line of code that was supposed to do otherwise. There is a probability mass that landed on the wrong side of a threshold nobody explicitly wrote.

This collapses the standard triage question. "Is this a bug or a feature?" presumes an author with intent. With a stochastic system, the honest answer is often "it is a tail event." The same prompt, resampled, produces a correct answer. The defect is not in the artifact. The defect is in the distribution, and the distribution is not something you can patch on a Tuesday.

Teams are responding by inventing a new category of ticket that fits none of the old buckets. It is not a crash. It is not a regression. It is a statistical complaint about a model's behavior under conditions the reporter may not have documented and cannot reliably reproduce.

## The Reproducibility Collapse

Classical bug triage runs on a simple contract: give me steps, I will reproduce it, I will fix it. That contract is now broken for a growing share of reports.

- **Non-determinism.** Temperature above zero, or any sampling, means the same input can yield different outputs. "Cannot reproduce" is not a dismissal. It is the expected result.
- **Prompt sensitivity.** A single added space or reordered clause changes the answer. The reporter's "steps to reproduce" are a description of what they typed, not a description of the state that produced the failure.
- **Version drift.** The model behind the endpoint may have been silently updated. The report references a behavior that no longer exists, or a behavior that never existed in the deployed version.
- **Context dependence.** Retrieval, system prompts, and conversation history all shape the output. The isolated repro is a different system than the one that failed.

Each of these turns a bug report into an argument about epistemics. What did you actually observe? How many times? Under what conditions? Did you record it? Most users did not, and most products do not make it easy to.

## The Stakes Are Not Abstract

This is not an academic taxonomy problem. It has teeth.

Regulators in the EU and elsewhere now ask firms to document how they evaluate and monitor AI systems. A bug tracker full of "model said something wrong" tickets with no reproducible evidence is a compliance liability. It is also a discovery nightmare. If a plaintiff's lawyer asks how many times your system produced a given harmful output, and your answer is "we don't know, we closed the tickets as cannot reproduce," that is a bad day.

Internally, the cost is subtler. Engineering time gets consumed by unfalsifiable reports. Product managers are asked to prioritize vibes. Trust erodes between the people who build the system and the people who use it, because neither side can point to a shared standard of correctness.

The teams handling this well are doing three things. They instrument aggressively, logging inputs, outputs, model versions, and sampling parameters so that a report can be replayed against the exact state that produced it. They define evaluation suites, not just unit tests, so that "the model is worse at X" becomes a measurable claim. And they separate the ticket queue: reproducible defects go to engineering, statistical behavior goes to an eval and monitoring pipeline, and policy questions go to the people who own acceptable use.

## The Philosophical Part Is the Practical Part

It is tempting to treat the "is this a bug" question as navel-gazing. It is the opposite. Every organization shipping probabilistic systems is now forced to answer, in writing, what counts as correct behavior, who decides, and how they would know if it changed. That is a specification problem wearing a philosophy costume.

The old bug report worked because someone could point at the code and say what it was supposed to do. That someone is now a committee, the code is a distribution, and the report is a claim about the world that may or may not survive resampling.

## Takeaway

The bug report did not become philosophical because engineers got pretentious. It became philosophical because the artifact lost its author. Teams that keep treating stochastic failures as ordinary defects will keep closing tickets they cannot fix. Teams that build evaluation, logging, and explicit standards of correctness will turn the same reports into signal. The ticket was never the point. The shared definition of "correct" was.
