The bug report said: "Search is broken. Please fix ASAP."

The developer opened it, read it twice, and moved on to the next ticket. She had no idea which search, which environment, which user, or what "broken" meant. Two days later the reporter pinged her manager. The bug was real. The report was useless.

A good bug report is a handoff document. It transfers a reproducible problem from someone who saw it to someone who can fix it, with as little back-and-forth as possible. Most bug reports fail because the writer describes the symptom from their own point of view and skips everything the engineer needs to reproduce it.

Here's the structure that works, and the specific information that turns a vague complaint into a ticket someone can act on today.

## Lead with what broke, not the backstory

The first line of your bug report should let a reader decide in five seconds whether this ticket is theirs.

Weak: "I was testing the checkout flow this morning after the deploy and noticed something odd when I tried to apply a discount code that I had set up last week for the holiday campaign."

Strong: "Discount codes over 20% are rejected at checkout with 'Invalid code' even when the code is active."

The weak version buries the finding in a paragraph of context. The strong version states the defect, the condition, and the observed error. Anyone scanning the backlog knows instantly whether they own this.

Use this pattern for the title: **[What's broken] when [condition] — [observed result]**. It's boring and it works. Engineers triage dozens of tickets a day. The title is often all they read before assigning.

## The six fields every bug report needs

You don't need a fancy template. You need six pieces of information, in this order.

**1. Summary.** One sentence describing the defect. Same as your title, expanded slightly.

**2. Steps to reproduce.** A numbered list someone can follow without guessing. This is the field people skip, and it's the one that determines whether your bug gets fixed this sprint or sits for a month.

**3. Expected behavior.** What should have happened.

**4. Actual behavior.** What did happen. Include the exact error message, not a paraphrase.

**5. Environment.** Browser, OS, app version, device, account type, and anything else that scopes the problem. "Production, Chrome 121, macOS 14, free-tier account" tells the engineer where to look. "The website" does not.

**6. Evidence.** Screenshot, screen recording, console log, network trace, or a link to the session replay. One good screenshot saves three Slack messages.

If you fill in only these six fields and nothing else, your report will outperform 90% of the tickets in the queue.

## Steps to reproduce are the whole ballgame

An engineer's job is to make the bug happen on their machine. If they can't, they can't fix it. Your steps are the bridge.

Write them like a recipe, starting from a clean state:

1. Log in as a free-tier user (test account: qa-free@example.com).
2. Add any item to the cart.
3. Go to checkout and enter discount code SAVE25.
4. Click Apply.

Four steps. No ambiguity about where to start or what to click. Compare that to "try to use a discount code" — which is a scavenger hunt, not a reproduction.

Three rules make steps reliable:

- **Start from a known state.** "Log out and log back in" or "clear cart" removes the variable of whatever the tester did five minutes earlier.
- **One action per line.** Combining "open settings and change the theme and reload" hides which step actually triggers the bug.
- **Include the data.** The exact discount code, the exact item, the exact account. "A discount code" is not reproducible. "SAVE25" is.

If you can reproduce the bug only sometimes, say so, and describe the pattern. "Happens about 1 in 5 attempts, more often on mobile Safari" is a valuable clue. Pretending it's consistent wastes everyone's time.

## Screenshots, logs, and the evidence that actually helps

A screenshot of an error message is table stakes. The evidence that moves tickets faster is the stuff most reporters don't think to grab.

**The browser console.** Open developer tools, reproduce the bug, and screenshot any red errors. A single line like `TypeError: Cannot read property 'id' of undefined` can point an engineer straight to the broken code.

**The network tab.** If a request fails, the response status and body tell the backend team whether the problem is theirs. Filter to failed requests and capture the response.

**Timestamps and user IDs.** "Happened at 2:14 PM ET on account 88213" lets an engineer pull the exact server logs. Without it, they're searching a haystack.

**A short screen recording.** For anything involving animation, timing, or a multi-step flow, a 15-second video beats a paragraph of description. Most operating systems record the screen with a keyboard shortcut. Use it.

Don't attach twelve screenshots of the same thing. One clear capture of the failure, plus the console or network evidence, is enough.

## Severity, priority, and who to tell

Severity and priority are different, and mixing them up gets your tickets deprioritized.

**Severity** is how bad the defect is in the abstract: does it crash the app, corrupt data, block a core flow, or just look wrong? **Priority** is how soon *this* team should fix it given everything else on the roadmap. A cosmetic typo on the homepage has low severity but might be high priority the day before a launch.

State your view, then let the team decide. "Severity: high — blocks checkout for all discount users. Priority: your call, but this is costing revenue every hour." That's a useful input. "URGENT!!! FIX NOW" is noise, and engineers learn to ignore it.

For anything that blocks a release or affects paying customers, don't rely on the ticket alone. Send a short message in the team channel with the ticket link and one line on impact. The ticket is the record. The message is the nudge. Use both.

Paste this into your issue tracker as a template and the quality of incoming reports jumps immediately. It takes about ninety seconds to fill in once you've reproduced the bug.

## The habit that makes reports better over time

Before you file a ticket, reproduce the bug one more time from a clean state, following your own steps exactly. If you can't, your steps are wrong. Fix them before you submit.

That single habit — reproduce it yourself using only what you wrote — catches most bad reports before they reach a developer. It's the difference between a ticket that gets fixed and a ticket that gets a comment asking for more information three days later.

The developer who fixed the "search is broken" bug eventually got what she needed, but only after two rounds of questions and a call. The next report from that same person led with the exact search query, the account, the console error, and a screen recording. It was fixed the same afternoon. Same reporter, same kind of bug, completely different outcome. The only thing that changed was the report.
