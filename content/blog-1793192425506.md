The transcript is 8,000 words. The meeting was 45 minutes. You need three things from it: the decision on the pricing test, who owns the migration, and the date you agreed on.

An AI notetaker can hand you a clean summary in seconds. It can also hand you a confident, well-formatted summary that gets the owner wrong. The difference between those two outcomes is almost never the tool. It's what you do in the ten minutes after the meeting ends.

Here's a working method for using AI to capture meetings without letting it quietly rewrite what happened.

## Decide what the meeting is for before you record it

Not every meeting deserves a transcript. Recording everything creates a searchable archive of decisions nobody made and action items nobody owns.

Use AI notes for meetings where the record matters later: planning sessions, customer calls, incident reviews, cross-team syncs where commitments get made. Skip them for standups, quick unblocks, and anything where the value is the live conversation and a two-line Slack post covers the rest.

One practical rule: if you'd be annoyed to reconstruct the outcome from memory next Thursday, record it. If not, don't.

## What AI does well, and what it quietly gets wrong

Modern notetakers are good at three things: capturing who said what, pulling out action items phrased as sentences, and giving you a timestamped transcript you can search. That's genuinely useful. Finding the exact moment a customer said "we'd pay for that" used to take twenty minutes of scrubbing audio.

They're unreliable at four things, and these are the ones that cause trouble:

**Attribution.** Speaker labels drift when people talk over each other or join late. A summary that says "the team agreed to delay the launch" when actually one person suggested it and nobody responded is worse than no summary. It reads as consensus.

**Hedging.** People say "we could probably do that by Friday if nothing else lands." The transcript keeps the hedge. The summary often drops it. Now you have a commitment that was never made.

**Decisions vs. discussion.** AI tends to flatten a 20-minute debate into a single line that sounds like an outcome. Sometimes the outcome was "we're not deciding today." The summary won't tell you that unless you check.

**Numbers and names.** Dates, dollar figures, and product names get transcribed wrong more often than you'd expect, especially with accents, acronyms, or internal jargon. Every number in an AI summary needs a human check.

## The five-minute review pass

Treat the AI summary as a first draft written by someone who was in the room but distracted. Your job is editing, not accepting.

Run this checklist before you send anything:

1. **Find the decision.** Search the transcript for "decided," "agreed," "let's go with," and "we'll do." If the summary lists a decision, confirm the transcript shows someone actually making it.
2. **Check every owner.** For each action item, confirm a named person accepted it. "Someone should look into this" is not an owner. If the summary invented one, fix it or mark it unassigned.
3. **Verify every date and number.** Cross-check against the transcript. If it's ambiguous, write "TBD" and flag it.
4. **Cut the recap.** AI summaries love restating context that everyone in the room already knows. Delete it. Your readers weren't there and don't need the setup — they need the outcome.
5. **Add what the tool missed.** Side conversations, a concern someone raised and dropped, the thing you agreed to offline. The tool only knows what the mic caught.

Five minutes of this beats an hour of rewriting from scratch, and it's the difference between notes people trust and notes people learn to ignore.

## Write the summary for people who weren't there

The transcript serves the people in the room. The summary serves everyone else: the stakeholder who skipped it, the engineer who joins next sprint, the person reading this in three months to figure out why the pricing test got shelved.

Structure it around what a reader needs, not what order things were discussed:

- **What we decided** — one line per decision, with the rationale in a sentence.
- **What changes** — the concrete next steps, each with an owner and a date.
- **What's still open** — the questions you didn't resolve, and who's chasing them.
- **Where to look** — a link to the transcript with timestamps for the two or three moments that matter.

That last item is the one people skip and later wish they hadn't. A timestamped link to the exact exchange saves the next person from re-reading 8,000 words.

## Keep a human in the loop, permanently

The failure mode with AI notes isn't bad transcription. It's the slow erosion of trust that happens when a summary says something happened that didn't, and nobody catches it until it's been forwarded three times. Once that happens, people stop reading the summaries and start asking you directly — which is exactly the work the tool was supposed to save.

So build the review into the process. One person owns the summary for each meeting. That person reads the draft, fixes the owners and dates, and hits send. The AI does the transcription and the first pass. You do the judgment.

That division of labor is the whole trick. The tool is fast and tireless and occasionally wrong in ways that sound confident. You're slower and you were actually there. Use each for what it's good at.
