It’s demo day. Your team just finished a two-week sprint, wrestling with a messy third-party API and a dozen edge cases. You pull up the staging environment, your cursor hovering over the new icon. You walk the stakeholders through the workflow you spent weeks designing, debating, and building. It solves a real, painful problem for your power users.

You finish the walkthrough, beaming. "And that's how we're cutting reporting time in half for our enterprise accounts."

You pause, ready for the wave of validation. The VP of Marketing unmutes. "This is great. Quick question... could we make that confirmation button green? Our brand guidelines just updated."

The air leaves the room. The engineer who stayed late all week to fix a backend bug just stares into the middle distance. The entire conversation has been successfully derailed from strategic value to cosmetic preference.

This isn't your stakeholder's fault. It's yours.

You presented a solution and invited feedback on the most obvious, superficial layer: the pixels. When you just show *what* you built, people give feedback on *what* they can see. Your job is to make the user's problem and the business's outcome the most concrete things in the room, not the UI.

A demo is not a design review. It's a closing argument. Here’s how to make your case and keep the conversation focused on what matters.

### Start Before the Meeting

The framing starts before anyone clicks the Zoom link. Your calendar invite is the first exhibit. Don't just send an invite titled "New Feature Demo." That sets zero context.

Instead, send a short, sharp pre-read the day before. It doesn't need to be a long document. It just needs to prime the audience.

**Template for a 1-Paragraph Pre-Read:**

> **Subject:** Demo & Feedback: New Automated Reporting Suite
>
> Team,
>
> Tomorrow at 10 AM, we'll be demoing the new automated reporting suite. As a reminder, the core problem we're solving is that our account managers spend ~3 hours per week manually exporting and combining CSVs for their top clients. Our goal with this V1 is to reduce that manual work to under 15 minutes.
>
> We're specifically looking for feedback on one question: *Does this workflow solve the core problem in a way that feels intuitive for a non-technical user?*

This does three things instantly. It restates the problem, defines the goal, and frames the exact feedback you need. You've already built guardrails against color commentary.

### The First 60 Seconds Dictate the Next 30 Minutes

Do not start your demo by saying, "Okay, so I'm on the dashboard..."

Start with the pain. Tell a story.

*   **Bad:** "Here's the new feature. You click here, and it opens a modal where you can select a date range."
*   **Good:** "I want to remind everyone of the call we had with Dave from Acme Inc. He showed us the spreadsheet he built, with 14 tabs, that he spends every Monday morning updating. His screen is about to get a lot simpler. Let's look at Dave's new Monday morning."

Grounding the demo in a specific user story makes the problem tangible. The audience isn't just looking at a UI; they're watching a problem get solved for a person. It shifts their perspective from "What do I think of this screen?" to "Does this help Dave?"

### Narrate the Value, Not the Clicks

As you move through the feature, connect every action back to the user's goal. Don't describe the UI; describe the user's journey to value.

*   **Instead of:** "We added a search bar here at the top."
*   **Say:** "Dave told us he always loses track of reports shared by his team, so we put a universal search right at the top to eliminate that hunting and pecking."

*   **Instead of:** "After you select the metrics, you hit 'Generate Report'."
*   **Say:** "This is the moment that replaces 45 minutes of VLOOKUPs for Dave. He's chosen his metrics, and with one click, the platform does all the work."

This technique forces the audience to evaluate each part of the feature based on its utility, not its aesthetics. You’re not just showing them a button; you're showing them a purpose-built tool for a specific job.

### Ask a Better Question

The most common demo mistake is ending with a weak, open-ended question. "Any questions?" or "So... what do you think?" is an invitation for random, unfocused feedback.

Just as you framed the meeting in the pre-read, you must frame the feedback at the end. Be specific. Direct their input toward the feedback you actually need.

*   "What's the biggest risk you see with this approach?"
*   "For the sales team, how does this change the conversation you can have with prospects who are struggling with this problem?"
*   "What's one thing that feels confusing or could trip a new user up?"
*   "Now that you've seen it, does this feel like it would genuinely save Dave three hours a week?"

These questions can't be answered with "make the button green." They demand that stakeholders think critically about the strategy, the user experience, and the business impact.

A demo is your chance to build conviction. Conviction that your team understood the problem, built the right thing, and is ready for the next challenge. Stop presenting a collection of screens and start presenting a solved problem. You’ll stop getting feedback on button colors and start getting the strategic buy-in you actually need.
