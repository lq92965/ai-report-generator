The incident channel for the checkout outage went quiet at 2:14 a.m., nine hours after the first 500s started. What followed wasn't a fix. It was a negotiation.

By the time the on-call engineer posted the timeline, three people had already edited it. The database team removed a line about a manual failover that took forty minutes because no one had run it since March. A director added a bullet about "unexpected traffic patterns" that no metric supported. The engineer who actually triggered the deploy — a contractor on a ninety-day engagement — was told, in a side thread, that his name could stay off the doc if he agreed the root cause was "configuration drift." He agreed. He needed the reference.

This is the new shape of the postmortem. It is no longer a document that explains what broke. It is a document that allocates blame, and the allocation is settled before the writing starts.

## The blameless ritual has a silent second half

The blameless postmortem was a real advance. When Etsy and Google popularized it in the 2010s, the logic was sound: if people fear punishment, they hide the details, and hidden details are how the same outage happens twice. Write down what happened, assume good faith, fix the system.

That logic still holds. What changed is the number of parties with something to lose. A decade ago a postmortem circulated among engineers who shared a manager. Now the same document is read by legal, by the customer success team drafting the credit memo, by a compliance officer mapping the incident to a contractual SLA, and by whoever is deciding next quarter's headcount. The blameless room has acquired spectators, and spectators change what people are willing to say.

I've watched this play out across a half-dozen companies. The tell is always the same: a timeline that is oddly precise about infrastructure and oddly vague about decisions. "A deploy was initiated at 17:42" — passive voice, no actor. "Alerting thresholds were not tuned for this scenario" — true, and also a sentence that could describe any system ever built. The document is technically honest and strategically empty.

## The contractor is the cheapest thing to blame

Look at who gets named in the postmortems that leak. It is rarely the person who approved the architecture, rarely the vendor whose library swallowed the error, rarely the executive who cut the SRE budget in the spring planning cycle. It is the most replaceable person in the chain, because blaming them costs the organization the least.

That is not a failure of engineering culture. It is a rational response to incentives. When a postmortem can be subpoenaed, when it feeds a performance review, when it decides whether a team keeps its headcount, the document stops being a learning tool and becomes evidence. Evidence gets curated. Curated evidence is a plea bargain dressed in the language of continuous improvement.

The contractor who kept his name off the doc? He was the one who noticed the connection pool was misconfigured three weeks earlier. He mentioned it in a Slack thread that nobody read. That thread is now the only place the real cause is written down, and it's buried under a thousand messages in a channel that will be archived in June.

## What a postmortem is actually for

Here is the uncomfortable part: the blameless postmortem was always partly a fiction. Someone usually could have prevented the outage. Systems fail because people make choices, and pretending otherwise produces documents that blame "the system" — an entity that cannot be fired, retrained, or held accountable, which is precisely why it is such a convenient defendant.

The better move isn't to abandon blamelessness. It's to be honest about the two audiences. The internal version, the one engineers use to actually learn, needs to name decisions and decision-makers, including the ones with titles. The external version, the one that goes to legal and customers, is a different artifact with different rules. Conflating them produces the worst of both: a document too sanitized to teach and too detailed to protect anyone.

The checkout outage got fixed by 4 a.m. The postmortem shipped eleven days later, after four rounds of edits, with the contractor's name absent and the failover gap described as "an opportunity for automation." Nobody who reads it will learn that a manual step went untested for four months because the team that owned it had been reassigned. The document is clean. The system is not.
