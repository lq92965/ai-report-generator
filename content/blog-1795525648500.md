Mira joined the payments team in March. By her second week she'd found the architecture doc, the roadmap, and a Slack channel with 40,000 messages. What she couldn't find was why the team had abandoned the multi-currency checkout redesign six months earlier. The code was gone, the ticket was closed as "won't do," and the only person who remembered the reason had left the company in January.

She spent three days reconstructing the story from fragments. Then she rebuilt the same proposal her predecessor had already killed — for the same reasons.

This is the most expensive reporting failure in product work, and it almost never shows up in a status report. Decisions evaporate. The reasoning behind them evaporates faster. Teams re-litigate settled questions, or worse, quietly reverse them because nobody remembers the tradeoff that made them.

A decision log fixes this. Not a wiki page nobody reads, not a Confluence graveyard — a short, maintained record of what you decided, why, and what would change your mind.

## What a decision log actually is (and isn't)

A decision log is a running list of consequential choices, each with four things: the decision, the rationale, the alternatives you rejected, and the trigger that would reopen it. That's it. One row per decision.

It is not a meeting minutes archive. Minutes record what people said. A decision log records what the team committed to and why. It is not a risk register, though the two often reference each other. And it is not a changelog — that tracks what shipped, not what you chose.

The distinction matters because most teams already have three places where decisions half-live: a Slack thread, a PRD comment, and someone's memory. The log's job is to be the one place where a decision is legible six months later to someone who wasn't in the room.

## The four fields that earn their keep

Every field you add is a field someone has to fill. Keep the schema tight or the log dies within a quarter. Here's the minimum that survives contact with a real team:

**Decision.** One sentence, past tense, specific. "We will ship the single-currency checkout in Q3 and defer multi-currency to Q1 next year." Not "checkout scope."

**Rationale.** Two or three sentences on the reasoning. Name the constraint that drove it — engineering capacity, a customer commitment, a regulatory deadline. This is the field people skip and the field that saves the most time later.

**Alternatives considered.** A bullet list of what you rejected and the one-line reason. This is what stops the re-litigation. When someone proposes the rejected option in eight months, you point at the row instead of re-running the debate.

**Reopen trigger.** The condition under which this decision should be revisited. "Reopen if we sign a customer with multi-currency as a hard requirement" or "Reopen at the Q1 planning cycle." This turns a decision from permanent into conditional, which is usually the truth.

Optional fifth field: **owner and date.** Cheap to add, useful when you need to ask a follow-up question. But if you're choosing between a log with four fields that gets maintained and one with seven that doesn't, pick four.

## Where to keep it

The tool matters less than the access pattern. The log needs to be findable in under thirty seconds by someone who doesn't know it exists. That rules out anything buried three folders deep.

Three options that work in practice:

- **A single table in your existing docs tool** (Notion, Confluence, Google Sheets). Best for most teams. Sortable, searchable, linkable from PRDs.
- **A dedicated Slack channel with a pinned index.** Works if your team lives in Slack, but decisions get lost in scroll unless someone maintains the index. Higher maintenance.
- **A folder of one-page decision records** (the ADR pattern, borrowed from engineering). Best for architecture-level decisions with long reasoning. Overkill for routine product calls.

Pick one and put the link in your team's onboarding doc, your PRD template, and your sprint planning notes. If a new hire can't find it in their first week, it doesn't exist.

## The maintenance ritual that keeps it alive

Logs die from neglect, not from bad design. The fix is a small, boring habit tied to something you already do.

**Log at the moment of decision, not after.** The decision is freshest in the room where it was made. Add a five-minute agenda item to the end of any meeting where a real choice got made: "Who's logging this?" Assign one person, not "the team." The log entry is due before the meeting recap goes out.

**Review the log at planning.** Once a month, scan the reopen triggers. Anything that's fired gets a fresh discussion. This is where the log pays for itself — it surfaces decisions that have quietly gone stale instead of letting them rot in the background.

**Prune ruthlessly.** A decision that's been superseded gets marked superseded, not deleted. A decision that turned out to be trivial can be archived. The log should stay short enough to read in one sitting. If it's over a couple hundred rows, split by product area or archive the closed ones.

The teams that keep decision logs for years share one trait: the log review is baked into a meeting they already have. No new ceremony, no new calendar invite. Just a standing item that takes ten minutes.

## A worked example

Here's what a real row looks like, filled in for the payments scenario:

> **Decision:** Defer multi-currency checkout to Q1 next year; ship single-currency in Q3.
> **Rationale:** Multi-currency requires a payments provider migration that the platform team can't start until Q4. Shipping single-currency unblocks the enterprise pilot committed for September.
> **Alternatives:** (1) Build multi-currency in-house — rejected, 2 engineers for a full quarter, no capacity. (2) Delay the enterprise pilot — rejected, contractual commitment.
> **Reopen trigger:** Reopen if the platform migration moves earlier, or if a second enterprise customer requires multi-currency before Q1.
> **Owner:** Priya, 2024-03-14.

Six months later, when someone proposes multi-currency again, this row answers the question in ten seconds. When the platform migration gets pulled forward, the reopen trigger fires and the team revisits on purpose instead of by accident.

## Why this beats a status report for institutional memory

Status reports answer "where are we?" Decision logs answer "why are we here?" The first is a snapshot that expires in a week. The second compounds.

The practical payoff shows up in three places. Onboarding gets faster because new hires read the log instead of interviewing five people. Re-planning gets cheaper because you're not re-debating settled questions. And escalations get shorter because when a stakeholder asks "why did you choose this?", you send a link instead of scheduling a call.

None of this requires new tooling or a big rollout. Start with the next decision your team makes. Write the four fields. Put the link somewhere findable. Add the review to your next planning meeting. The log will be thin for a month and indispensable by the end of the quarter.

Mira's three days of archaeology would have been a ten-minute read. That's the whole argument.
