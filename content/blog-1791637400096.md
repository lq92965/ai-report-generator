The metrics were clear. The leadership team agreed. You made the call. The feature that consumed your team for the last two quarters is being sunsetted. You close the epic in Jira, archive the Slack channel, and feel a brief, sharp pang of relief.

Then the DM arrives from your tech lead. "So, was the last six months of our work for nothing?"

Your stomach drops. You thought the hard part was making the decision. The hard part is communicating it to the people who poured their creativity and late nights into building the very thing you just killed. A terse announcement in the company-wide channel won't cut it. "To align with our new strategic priorities, we will be deprecating the Project Dashboard on May 31st." That kind of corporate-speak is a morale killer. It’s technically true but emotionally hollow. It leaves a vacuum that your team will fill with resentment and cynicism.

You don't need a press release. You need an obituary. A thoughtful, internal document that honors the work, explains the decision with data, and realigns the team on the path forward.

### Acknowledge the Ghost in the Machine

Before you talk about the future, you have to acknowledge the past. Your team didn't just ship code; they shipped a piece of themselves. They argued over API designs in a conference room, they hunted down a production bug on a Sunday, they felt a surge of pride at the demo.

Your obituary must honor that effort. Start there.

*   **Don't say:** "The team worked hard on the Project Dashboard."
*   **Do say:** "I want to start by thanking the entire Tiger Team for their incredible work on the Project Dashboard. From Maria’s brilliant UX flows to Chen’s elegant data pipeline, this feature was a feat of engineering and design. We delivered it on time and bug-free."

Specific praise shows you were paying attention. It validates the effort, separating the quality of the work from the outcome of the feature.

### Re-state the Original, Noble Goal

No one sets out to build something useless. You built this feature because you had a strong hypothesis. Remind everyone of that optimistic starting point. What problem did you believe you were solving for customers?

This frames the project not as a mistake, but as a calculated bet based on the information you had at the time. It was a worthy quest.

*   **Don't say:** "The feature was intended to improve user productivity."
*   **Do say:** "When we kicked this off, our research showed that users were struggling to track cross-project dependencies. Our hypothesis was that a centralized dashboard would save them hours each week. We believed we could become their single source of truth."

### Present the Data as the Decision-Maker

You aren't the villain; the data is the impartial judge. Now, you present the evidence. This is the critical moment where you shift from emotion to rationale. Be transparent and direct.

Show the charts. Share the numbers.

*   Adoption rate: "While we found a passionate group of 500 weekly active users who love the dashboard, it never broke out to the wider user base. After six months, it had a 2% adoption rate among our target accounts."
*   Strategic cost: "The dashboard requires constant maintenance from two engineers to support its complex data integrations. This is pulling our most senior backend talent away from the new AI initiative, which our Q3 strategy depends on."

The feature didn't fail. The hypothesis was invalidated at scale. The cost of maintaining it now outweighs its benefit. This isn't an opinion; it's a business reality.

### Connect the End to a New Beginning

Sunsetting a feature creates a void. You must immediately fill it with a compelling vision for what's next. This is the most important step. You are not just closing a door; you are opening a new one.

Show the team where their liberated energy and talent will now be focused.

*   **Don't say:** "This will free up resources for other projects."
*   **Do say:** "By making this tough call, the entire Tiger Team is now free to focus on what we all agree is the biggest opportunity for the company: building out the AI co-pilot. Their deep expertise in our data architecture is exactly what we need to make that project a massive success. The kickoff is next week."

You aren't firing a team; you're redeploying your best soldiers to the most important front. Killing a feature feels like a failure. But communicating it with honesty and respect turns a painful moment into a defining act of leadership. It builds trust that will last far longer than any single feature.
