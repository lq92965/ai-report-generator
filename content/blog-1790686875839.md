You hit “Share” on the Google Doc and lean back. The PRD is crisp. The problem statement is tight. You shared it with engineering, design, marketing, and sales, asking for their async feedback by Friday. Productive.

Then you check back on Thursday.

It’s a bloodbath of colored text. Your lead engineer has left 17 comments suggesting a different technical approach, effectively cutting the scope in half. The designer is questioning the core user persona in a long comment thread with another designer. Marketing wants to know if you can add three more tracking events. And two salespeople are having a conversation with each other in the margins about a feature a competitor just launched.

You’re not a product manager anymore. You’re a UN peacekeeper, and your job for the next two days is to negotiate truces in comment threads.

## Your Process Optimized for Input, Not Agreement

Async feedback is a powerful tool for catching typos, clarifying requirements, and correcting factual errors. It’s a terrible tool for building consensus on anything that involves trade-offs.

When stakeholders comment in isolation, they provide their own perspective without the context of others' constraints. The engineer sees a chance to reduce complexity. The marketer sees a chance to get more data. The designer sees a chance to perfect the UX. Each piece of feedback is logical on its own, but together they create a set of contradictory demands.

You asked for feedback, and you got a collection of individual wishlists. Now the burden is on you to synthesize, sequence, and reject them—all while trying to make everyone feel heard. This isn’t collaboration. It’s asynchronous whack-a-mole.

## A Better Way: The Feedback Funnel

The goal isn't to eliminate async feedback. It's to stage it correctly. You need to separate feedback on the *problem* from feedback on the *solution*, and feedback for *clarification* from feedback for *decision-making*.

Think of it as a funnel with three distinct stages.

### Stage 1: Broad Async for Comprehension

The first time you share a doc, your goal is simple: Does everyone understand the problem and the goals? That's it. You need to be ruthlessly explicit about what kind of feedback you want.

**Your Ask:** "Please review this draft by EOD tomorrow. I'm specifically looking for feedback on two things: 1) Is the problem statement clear and accurate? 2) Are there any major factual errors or gaps in the 'Background' section? Please hold all feedback on solutions or scope for now."

This prevents the immediate jump to solutioning. It forces everyone to agree on the "what" and "why" before they argue about the "how." If someone jumps the gun, you gently redirect them: "Great thought on the solution. Let's park that for the workshop. For now, does the problem as I've written it make sense to you?"

### Stage 2: A Real-Time Workshop for Debate

This is where the magic happens. Once everyone agrees on the problem, you get the core decision-makers in a room (or a video call) for 45 minutes. This group should be small—the engineering lead, design lead, and key business stakeholder.

The agenda is not "Let's review the doc." The doc is the pre-read. The agenda is "Decide on the 3 biggest trade-offs for the MVP."

This is where you debate the hard stuff. Can we ship without that integration? Is the simpler design good enough for V1? What's the biggest risk here? The goal of this meeting is not to talk; it's to decide. You are the facilitator, forcing clarity on the choices and their consequences. The decisions are captured live in a simple table at the top of the PRD.

### Stage 3: Targeted Async for Execution Details

Now the core debates are settled and the decisions are made. You can go back to the wider group for a final round of async feedback.

**Your Ask:** "Thanks for the initial feedback. Based on our workshop, we've decided on the core scope and trade-offs for the MVP (see 'Decisions' table on page 1). Please review the updated spec by EOD Friday. I'm now looking for feedback on implementation details, missing edge cases, or unclear acceptance criteria."

This round is for polish, not for relitigating the strategy. Because the major decisions are already documented, people are less likely to reopen settled debates. Their feedback becomes more tactical and more useful.

Stop using a single "request for feedback" and expecting a coherent outcome. Guide your stakeholders through a structured process. Use async communication for clarity and a real-time meeting for debate. You’ll spend less time refereeing comments and more time building products.
