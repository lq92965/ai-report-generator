It’s demo day. The engineer pulls up the new data import feature. You watch as they upload a CSV file with a few errors in it. The spinner spins, then stops. A small success toast pops up: "Import complete. 5 of 7 records imported."

Wait. That’s not right.

"What happens to the two records that failed?" you ask.

The engineer looks confused. "They're dropped. We log the error on the back end."

Your designer chimes in. "I thought we were showing a modal that let the user fix the errors inline."

A knot forms in your stomach. You pull up the PRD you wrote three weeks ago. There it is, one bullet point under "Requirements": *The system should gracefully handle import errors.*

You thought "gracefully" was clear. The engineer read it as "don't crash the system." The designer read it as "provide a seamless user recovery path." You spent the next hour in a tense meeting, whiteboarding a solution that should have been in the spec. The feature is delayed. A week of engineering work was based on a guess.

### Your Weasel Words Are Wrecking Your Roadmap

We all use them. Words like "flexible," "intuitive," "performant," "stable," and "robust." They feel safe. They give us wiggle room. But these words aren't requirements; they are invitations for interpretation. Every person on your team projects their own assumptions and biases onto that single, vague word.

The engineer, focused on system stability, builds the simplest thing that won't break. The designer, focused on user experience, imagines an elegant, interactive flow. The QA tester, focused on edge cases, has a completely different mental model. You just created three different products with one lazy adjective.

The cost isn't just a heated meeting. It's wasted code, thrown-away designs, and a demoralized team that feels like it’s constantly guessing. The most expensive problems don't start with a line of bad code. They start with a line of ambiguous text.

### How to Write Requirements That Can't Be Misunderstood

Your job isn't to write a 50-page technical specification. Your job is to eliminate ambiguity at the most critical points. A PRD is a contract for collaboration. Make the terms clear.

**1. Define Every Adjective with a Metric.**
If you find yourself writing a subjective adjective, stop. Immediately follow it with an objective, measurable outcome.

*   **Instead of:** "The dashboard should load quickly."
*   **Write:** "The P95 load time for the dashboard, measured from click to render, must be under 2 seconds."

*   **Instead of:** "The design should be intuitive."
*   **Write:** "A new user, with no training, must be able to successfully create their first project in under 3 minutes in 80% of usability tests."

This forces you to define what success actually looks like. It transforms a vague hope into a testable hypothesis.

**2. Describe the "When/Then" of an Interaction.**
Don't just state what the system *should do*. Describe the specific trigger and the specific outcome. Use examples. This is the heart of good acceptance criteria.

*   **Instead of:** "The form should validate user input."
*   **Write:** "When the user enters an email address without an '@' symbol and clicks 'Submit,' then the email input field will be outlined in red and the helper text 'Please enter a valid email address' will appear below it."

This leaves zero room for interpretation. The engineer knows exactly what to build. The tester knows exactly what to check.

**3. Pressure-Test with "What Ifs".**
Your PRD is a theory. Before you ship it, you need to attack it. Get your tech lead and design lead in a room for 30 minutes and play devil's advocate. Your only job is to ask "what if" questions.

*   "We want to support CSV uploads. What if the file is 10GB?"
*   "The user can add team members. What if they try to add someone who is already in a different team?"
*   "The feature relies on a third-party API. What if that API is down or returns an error?"

These questions aren't meant to make you write down every possible edge case. They are meant to uncover the biggest assumptions hiding in your requirements. A 15-minute "what if" session can save you a week of rework later.

Clarity is a feature. It's the most important one you can build into your process. Stop writing documents that are open to interpretation and start writing blueprints that your team can build on with confidence. The sprint you save is your own.
