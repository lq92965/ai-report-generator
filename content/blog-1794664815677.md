The project is three weeks behind. Two engineers quit. The vendor missed a deadline and the fix has no date. You open a blank doc to write the update, and every sentence you draft sounds like a confession.

Most PMs write these updates badly because they treat them as a status confession. They lead with the bad news, bury it under a wall of context, or soften it until the reader can't tell what's actually wrong. Then leadership finds out anyway — from a Slack message, a hallway conversation, or the customer.

A good update during a troubled project does the opposite. It tells the reader what changed, what you're doing about it, and what you need from them. That's it. The format below keeps you honest and keeps your readers calm.

## Lead with the delta, not the diary

The instinct is to narrate: here's the original plan, here's what happened, here's the current state. Readers who are already worried don't need the history. They need to know what's different from what they last heard.

Start with a single line: **"Since the last update, [X] changed."** Not "the project remains on track with some risks." Not "we've had a few challenges." A concrete delta.

> Since the last update, the payments integration slipped from April 12 to an unknown date. The vendor's API doesn't support refunds, and we need that for launch.

That's the whole opening. Anyone who reads only that line knows the situation. Everyone who keeps reading gets the detail.

## Separate what you know from what you're guessing

During a crisis, updates get fuzzy because writers mix confirmed facts with speculation. "We think the vendor can fix it by Friday" reads the same as "the vendor confirmed Friday." That ambiguity is what triggers escalations — a leader reads a hedge as a promise.

Use two headings: **Confirmed** and **Open questions.** Under Confirmed, list only things you can point to a source for. Under Open questions, list what you're still chasing and who owns the chase.

| Confirmed | Open questions |
|---|---|
| Vendor's API has no refund endpoint | Can they add one? (asked Tue, no reply) |
| Refund flow blocks launch | Is a manual workaround acceptable for v1? (owner: Priya) |
| Two engineers reassigned off project | Backfill start date? (owner: you) |

The table does more than any paragraph could. It shows you're not panicking — you're tracking. And it makes clear which items have owners and which don't.

## State the ask, not just the problem

Here's the mistake I see most often. The update describes the problem in detail, then ends with "we'll keep you posted." That leaves the reader with nothing to do and a vague sense of dread.

Every update on a troubled project needs one explicit ask. It might be a decision, a resource, or just acknowledgment that you're handling it. Pick one and name it.

> **Ask:** I need a decision by Thursday on whether manual refunds are acceptable for v1. If yes, we hold the launch date. If no, we push two weeks.

Now the reader knows exactly what happens next and what they owe you. The update stops being a report and becomes a decision request — which is what you actually wanted.

## Keep the length tied to the size of the problem

A small slip needs three lines. A launch-threatening problem needs a page. What it never needs is padding to look thorough.

If your update is longer than the reader's attention span, they'll skim and miss the ask. The Confirmed/Open/Ask structure above fits in 150 words for most issues. If you need more, add a short "Background" section at the bottom for anyone who wants it — but don't make the main body longer than it needs to be.

## What to leave out

Three things don't belong in a troubled-project update:

- **Blame.** "The vendor dropped the ball" reads as venting. "The vendor's API lacks a refund endpoint" reads as a fact. Same event, different update.
- **Reassurance without evidence.** "I'm confident we'll get there" is noise unless you can say why. If you can, say why. If you can't, don't say it.
- **Every detail.** Your reader doesn't need the ticket numbers, the meeting transcript, or the timeline of who said what when. They need the delta, the facts, the open questions, and the ask.

## A template you can fill in ten minutes

Copy this into your next update:

```
**Since the last update:** [one sentence on what changed]

**Confirmed:**
- [fact you can source]
- [fact you can source]

**Open questions:**
- [question] — owner: [name], asked [date]
- [question] — owner: [name], asked [date]

**Ask:** [the one decision or resource you need], by [date].
```

That's it. Ten minutes to write, two minutes to read, and no ambiguity about what's happening or what the reader should do.

## The habit that makes these updates easy

The reason troubled-project updates are hard to write is that most PMs write them from memory on Friday afternoon. By then, the facts are fuzzy and the ask feels awkward.

Keep a running log during the week — one line per event, dated. When Friday comes, the update writes itself: the delta is the top of the log, the confirmed facts are the ones with sources, the open questions are the ones still unresolved. You're not reconstructing a week. You're transcribing a log.

The updates that build trust aren't the ones that hide bad news well. They're the ones that show a reader, in thirty seconds, that you see the problem clearly, you're tracking it, and you know what you need. That's the whole job.
