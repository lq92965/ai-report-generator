# The AI Is Eating Its Own Tail

The web is filling up with synthetic text, and the models trained on it are starting to show the symptoms of a bad diet. This is not a hypothetical. It is measurable, and it is already happening inside the pipelines of the companies selling you the next generation of intelligence.

The mechanism is simple enough to explain over coffee. Large language models learn by predicting the next token in a sequence. Feed them human writing and they absorb human patterns. Feed them output from other models and they absorb the artifacts of those models: the smoothed-out phrasing, the hedge-everything caution, the telltale rhythm of a system optimizing for plausibility rather than truth. Do this for a few training cycles and the distribution collapses. Rare words vanish. Weird but correct phrasings disappear. What remains is a narrow, self-referential mush that performs well on benchmarks and badly on reality.

Researchers have a name for the failure mode. Model collapse. The first rigorous demonstrations landed in 2023 and 2024, and the results were not subtle. Train a model mostly on its own generations and the tails of the distribution get amputated. Accuracy on minority cases degrades first. Then the model starts confidently reproducing its own errors as if they were facts. The process is gradual, which is what makes it dangerous. You do not get a dramatic failure. You get a slow drift toward mediocrity that looks fine on a dashboard.

## The Web Is Already Contaminated

The clean-data assumption that underpinned the first wave of LLMs is dead. Estimates vary, but a substantial and growing share of newly published web text is machine-generated. SEO farms churn out articles at industrial scale. Content marketing teams use models to draft, then publish with light edits. Forum posts, product reviews, and social replies are increasingly synthetic. The crawl that feeds the next training run is not the human web of 2015. It is a hybrid, and the synthetic fraction is climbing.

This creates a feedback loop with real economic stakes. Labs need fresh human text to keep improving. The supply of fresh human text is shrinking as the web fills with machine output. The obvious fix, licensing high-quality human corpora, is expensive and legally messy. So labs do what any rational actor does under scarcity: they filter, deduplicate, and hope. Filtering helps, but it is imperfect. Every filter that removes synthetic text also removes some human text that looks synthetic. You cannot cleanly separate the two at scale.

## Why This Is Not Just a Technical Curiosity

The consequences show up in places that matter.

- **Benchmarks become meaningless.** If test sets are contaminated with model output, high scores reflect memorization of synthetic patterns, not capability. Buyers making procurement decisions on those numbers are flying blind.
- **Minority languages and dialects erode first.** Low-resource languages have less human text to begin with, so synthetic contamination hits them harder and faster. The models get worse at exactly the languages that need help most.
- **Enterprise fine-tuning inherits the rot.** Companies fine-tuning on their own documents are fine until those documents include AI-drafted content, which increasingly they do. The degradation is invisible until a customer-facing failure makes it visible.
- **The moat shifts to data provenance.** The labs that win the next cycle may not be the ones with the best architecture. They may be the ones with the cleanest, best-documented, most defensibly human training data.

## The Uncomfortable Part

There is a version of this story where the problem solves itself. Better synthetic data filtering, synthetic data that is deliberately diverse, reinforcement learning from human feedback that corrects drift. Some of that is real. Some of it is wishful thinking dressed up as a roadmap.

The honest read is that we are running an experiment on the global information ecosystem with no control group and no rollback. The models are not going to stop producing text. The web is not going to stop filling with it. The question is whether the industry can build enough provenance infrastructure, pay enough for human data, and resist the temptation to cut corners on training corpora before the degradation becomes the default rather than the exception.

The labs know this. Several have quietly stopped disclosing what is in their training data, which is convenient when the answer is increasingly awkward. Transparency was never their strong suit, and the incentives now point hard toward opacity.

**The takeaway:** AI companies are burning through the human-written web faster than they can replace it, and the models are starting to eat their own output. The fix is expensive, unglamorous, and requires admitting that data quality, not parameter count, is the binding constraint. The industry has roughly one more training cycle to get this right before model collapse stops being a research paper and starts being a product defect.
