The weekly status report is the most-ignored document in most companies. That's not because people are lazy. It's because most weekly reports are written for the writer, not the reader. They're a diary of activity — "worked on X, had calls about Y, continuing Z" — that forces the reader to do the synthesis the writer should have done.

I've read hundreds of these as a PM and written more than I'd like to admit. The ones that get read share a structure. The ones that get skipped share a failure mode: they report motion instead of outcome.

Here's the method I use now, and the template you can copy.

## The core problem: activity is not progress

A status report that says "spent 12 hours on the migration" tells your reader nothing they can act on. Did the migration get closer to done? Is it blocked? Did you discover it's twice the size you thought?

Your reader — a manager, a stakeholder, a peer team — is scanning for three things: *is this on track, what changed, and do I need to do anything.* If your report doesn't answer those in the first ten seconds, it fails regardless of how much effort went into writing it.

So the whole method boils down to one rule: **lead with the state of things, not the list of things you did.**

## The structure that survives the inbox

Four sections. That's it. Keep the whole thing under 250 words unless something is genuinely on fire.

### 1. Status line (one sentence)

Open with a single sentence that states the trajectory. Not "here's what happened this week" — the actual verdict.

- **Green:** "On track — the payments integration ships Thursday as planned."
- **Yellow:** "Slipping two days — the vendor API changed and we're re-testing."
- **Red:** "Blocked — waiting on legal sign-off for the data-sharing agreement."

If you can't pick a color, you don't know your own status, and no amount of prose will fix that. Pick one. A wrong-but-honest yellow is worth more than a vague green.

### 2. What changed since last week

This is the section people skip and it's the one that matters most. Not what you did — what's *different* now that wasn't true seven days ago.

- A decision got made.
- A risk materialized or cleared.
- A date moved.
- A dependency landed or got stuck.

Three to five bullets, each stating the change and its consequence. "Chose Postgres over Dynamo for the audit log — adds a day of setup, removes the consistency risk we flagged." That's a change with a consequence. A reader can act on it.

### 3. What I need from you

Name the person and the ask. "Need Priya to confirm the pricing copy by Wednesday so design can lock the mockups." Not "waiting on pricing." If you need nothing, say "No blockers — nothing needed from you this week." That sentence is a gift to a busy reader; it tells them they can stop reading.

One caveat: don't use this section to relitigate decisions or assign blame. It's a request list, not a complaint box.

### 4. Next week's focus (two or three items max)

The top two or three things you'll actually move forward. This sets expectations and gives your reader a chance to redirect you *before* you spend a week on the wrong thing. If you list eight priorities, you've listed none.

## A copy-paste template

```
**Status: [Green/Yellow/Red]** — [one sentence verdict]

**What changed:**
- [Decision/risk/date/dependency] → [consequence]
- ...

**What I need:**
- [Name]: [specific ask] by [date]
- (or: No blockers.)

**Next week:**
- [Top priority 1]
- [Top priority 2]
```

Fill this in on Friday afternoon in ten minutes. If it takes you an hour, you're either writing the wrong report or you don't know your status — and the second problem is worth surfacing.

## Three habits that separate a good report from a great one

**Write it for the person who reads forty of these.** Your manager skims. Your skip-level skims harder. Front-load the verdict and the ask so a reader who stops after two lines still knows whether to worry.

**Report the risk before it becomes the incident.** A status report's real value is early warning. If you flag "the vendor contract renews in three weeks and we haven't started negotiating" while there's still time to act, you've done your most valuable work of the week. A report that only contains good news is a report nobody trusts.

**Keep a running draft all week.** Don't reconstruct Friday from memory — you'll forget the decision you made Tuesday and only remember the meetings. Jot changes into the draft as they happen. By Friday the report writes itself.

## The test: could a reader act on it without asking you a question?

That's the bar. If your report leaves a stakeholder wondering "so are we on track?" or "is there anything I need to do?", you've just created work instead of removing it. The four-section structure above exists to answer those two questions before they're asked.

Write the verdict first. State what changed. Name what you need. Then close the laptop and go enjoy your weekend — the report's already done its job.
