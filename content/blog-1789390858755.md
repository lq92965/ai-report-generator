The Slack notification pops up. It’s the VP of Sales. "Hey, quick question..." You know it's never a quick question. Three messages later, you’re looking at a screenshot of a competitor’s feature with the caption, "This. We need this. A customer will sign a huge deal if we have it."

Your stomach sinks. This isn't on the roadmap. It doesn't align with the quarterly goals. But a VP is asking, and a deal is on the line.

This is the backchannel. It’s the stream of "quick questions" in DMs, the "one more thing" at the end of a meeting, the drive-by idea in the hallway. It’s a shadow backlog that lives in your inbox and your brain, and it's derailing your team's focus. Your job isn’t to shut it down. Your job is to build a proper intake valve.

## The High Cost of Ad-Hoc Requests

When every idea comes through a different channel, you aren't a product manager; you're an air traffic controller in a snowstorm. Good ideas get lost in Slack threads. Bad ideas, championed by the loudest person in the room, get undeserved attention.

This chaos has real consequences:
*   **Team Whiplash:** Engineers get pulled off planned work to investigate the "urgent" flavor of the week. Context switching kills momentum and morale.
*   **Opaque Prioritization:** Decisions start to feel political. The team can't see why one idea gets picked over another, because the process is hidden in your DMs. Trust erodes.
*   **You Become the Bottleneck:** Every request has to be manually triaged, translated, and routed by you. You spend your day reacting instead of shaping the strategy.

You can't just tell people to stop having ideas. You need to give that energy a place to go.

## Build Your Intake Valve in 3 Steps

An intake valve is a single, official, and transparent system for capturing all inbound requests and ideas. It’s not about bureaucracy; it’s about clarity. Here’s how to build one.

### 1. Create a Single Front Door

First, decide where all requests will live. The tool is less important than the consistency. It could be a simple Trello board, a dedicated Slack channel (`#product-ideas`), a Google Form, or a Coda doc.

The goal is to make it dead simple for anyone to submit an idea the *right* way. Put the link in your Slack profile. Pin it in relevant channels. Talk about it constantly. This is the new front door. Everything else is a fire escape, to be used only in emergencies.

When the inevitable DM arrives, your response becomes automatic and polite: "This is great, thank you! To make sure this doesn't get lost, could you pop it into our intake form here? [LINK] That’s our single source of truth for all new ideas."
