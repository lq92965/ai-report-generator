A VP pings you at 4:52 on a Thursday: "Quick one — where are we on the migration?" You paste the link to last week's progress report. She replies: "I read that. I still don't know if we'll hit the date."

That exchange is the whole job. A progress report isn't a diary of what your team did. It's a forecast your reader can act on. Most progress reports fail because they answer "what happened?" when the reader is asking "what should I do now?"

Here's how to write one that answers the second question.

## Lead with the forecast, not the history

Your reader's first question is always some version of "will this land on time, and what's the risk?" Answer it in the first two sentences. Everything else is supporting evidence.

Bad opening:
> This week the team continued work on the data migration. We completed 14 of 22 tables, held three syncs, and resolved several blockers.

Good opening:
> The migration will hit the March 14 date if the vendor API fix lands by Feb 28. If it slips a week, we lose the buffer and the date moves to March 21. No decision needed from you yet — I'll flag it Feb 27 if the fix isn't in.

Same facts. The second version lets the VP close the tab. The first forces her to schedule a meeting to ask you what it means.

## The five-block structure that works

A progress report that survives being forwarded to someone who wasn't in the room has five blocks. Keep the whole thing under 300 words unless the reader asked for more.

**1. Status and forecast (2–3 sentences).** On track, at risk, or off track — and the date you expect, stated as a date, not a vibe. If it's at risk, name the specific thing that has to happen for the date to hold.

**2. What changed since last report.** Only changes. Not "we continued work on X." What moved, what broke, what got decided. If nothing changed, say "no material change" and skip to the risks. That's a complete report for a quiet week.

**3. Risks with owners and dates.** Each risk gets three things: what could go wrong, who's watching it, and the date you'll know. "Vendor API fix delayed — Priya owns — decision point Feb 27." A risk without a date is a worry, not a risk.

**4. Decisions needed.** If you need something from the reader, say what, by when, and what happens if they don't decide. If you need nothing, write "no decisions needed." That line is worth more than it looks — it tells the reader they can stop reading.

**5. Next milestone.** The next date the reader should care about, and what will be true when it's hit. One line.

## Write the risk section like a weather forecast

The riskiest part of any progress report is the risk section, because it's where writers get vague. "Timeline risk: medium" tells the reader nothing.

Use a probability-and-date frame instead:

- **Likely (70%+):** the vendor fix lands late. Impact: March 21 instead of March 14. Mitigation: Priya has a fallback adapter scoped at 3 days.
- **Possible (30–70%):** the QA environment stays flaky. Impact: two days of testing slip. Mitigation: we're running tests in the staging clone starting Monday.
- **Unlikely (<30%):** the data team's schema change breaks the loader. Impact: unknown until we test. Mitigation: spike scheduled for next Tuesday.

Readers calibrate against numbers. "Medium risk" gets ignored; "70% chance we slip a week" gets a reply.

## Show progress in units your reader cares about

Percent complete is the most misleading number in reporting. "60% done" on a migration means nothing — 60% of tables? 60% of rows? 60% of the work by hours, where the last 40% is the hard part?

Pick a unit the reader can verify and that maps to the finish line:

- **Counts:** 14 of 22 tables migrated, 8 remaining, 3 of those are the large ones.
- **Milestones:** 4 of 6 phases complete; phase 5 starts Monday and takes 9 days.
- **Burn-down:** 38 open items, down from 61 two weeks ago, closing at ~11/week.

Then add one sentence of interpretation: "At the current rate we finish the remaining tables by March 10, four days before the deadline." The number is evidence. The interpretation is the report.

## The one-line test before you send

Read your report and ask: if the reader only reads the first paragraph, do they know whether to worry? If the answer is "they'd have to read further," rewrite the first paragraph.

This is the same discipline behind a status report's RAG line — the color has to be earned by a sentence, not assigned by mood. A progress report's opening is that sentence.

## A template you can paste

```
**Status:** [On track / At risk / Off track] — expected [date]
[One sentence on what has to be true for the date to hold.]

**Since last report:**
- [Change 1]
- [Change 2]
- (or "No material change.")

**Risks:**
- [Risk] — [Likely/Possible/Unlikely], impact [X], [owner], decision point [date]

**Decisions needed:**
- [Decision] by [date], or [consequence of no decision]
- (or "None.")

**Next milestone:** [Date] — [what will be true]
```

Fill it in for a real project this week. Time yourself. The first one takes 20 minutes because you have to go find the numbers. The fifth takes five, because you've built the habit of tracking the unit that matters.

## What to leave out

Three things that pad most progress reports and help no one:

- **Activity lists.** "Held 4 syncs, reviewed 12 PRs" describes effort, not progress. If the reader wanted a timesheet, they'd ask.
- **Hedges without dates.** "Monitoring closely" and "keeping an eye on" mean nothing without a date you'll report back.
- **Apologies.** "Sorry for the delay in sending this" eats the reader's attention before the substance arrives. Send it late. Send it short.

The VP at 4:52 doesn't want a longer report. She wants one she can read in 30 seconds and either act on or close. Give her the forecast, the risk with a date, and the decision she owes you. That's the whole report.
