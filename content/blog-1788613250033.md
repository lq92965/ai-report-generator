You hit “share” on the Google Doc and post the link in Slack. “Team, here’s the one-pager on the new onboarding flow. Would love your feedback!”

You feel productive. It’s all in there: the problem statement from the user interviews, three bullet points on market size, a link to the competitive analysis spreadsheet, a rough user flow, and even a low-fi mockup from Figma. It’s a complete picture.

Then the comments start rolling in. Engineering asks about the feasibility of Step 3. Marketing questions the TAM data. Your design lead wants to know if you’re married to that specific UI pattern. An executive drive-by comment asks, “Have we considered a completely different approach?”

Twenty-four hours and fifty comments later, you have no clarity. You have more questions, more threads to pull, and zero alignment. Your one-pager, designed to create clarity, has manufactured confusion.

The problem isn’t the length. The problem is the purpose. You wrote a brain dump, a scrapbook of all your thinking. What your team needed was a decision memo.

### Your Brain Dump Invites Discussion, Not Decision

A brain-dump document has no clear objective. It presents information and implicitly asks the reader, “What do you think of all this stuff?” This approach fails for three reasons:

1.  **It lacks a sharp point.** Without a specific question to answer, everyone will focus on a different part of the document. The designer focuses on design, the engineer on engineering. You’ve invited them to a buffet, and they’re all eating different meals.
2.  **It creates cognitive overhead.** You’ve transferred the work of synthesis to your readers. You’re asking a dozen busy people to read through your research, connect the dots, and figure out what matters. That’s your job.
