It’s 4 PM on a Tuesday. You’re trying to lock down the requirements for the new billing page. You have a vague memory of the Head of Sales mentioning a critical compliance detail. Was it in a Slack DM last week? Or a comment on that completely unrelated Jira ticket? Maybe it was in the 15-email thread with the subject line "Re: Quick Question."

You spend the next 45 minutes digging through digital conversations, piecing together context like an archaeologist. The feature spec isn’t a document; it’s a distributed system of half-remembered conversations and out-of-context screenshots.

This isn’t just disorganized. It’s dangerous. The scavenger hunt for requirements actively undermines your product. When input is scattered across Slack, email, Jira, and Google Docs, you aren’t just wasting time. You’re shipping risk. A single forgotten Slack message can contain the one requirement that sinks the entire feature post-launch. A critical piece of feedback from a support ticket gets lost in the noise, only to resurface as a P0 fire a month later.

### The Illusion of "Being Available"

Most product managers fall into this trap with good intentions. We want to be accessible. We tell our stakeholders, "Just send me your ideas!" We pride ourselves on being responsive, whether the request comes via a text message or a hallway conversation.

But this open-door policy creates chaos. By accepting input from anywhere, at any time, in any format, you become a human router. Your primary job shifts from strategic decision-making to tactical information collation. And you will inevitably miss something. When a stakeholder’s idea gets dropped, they don’t think the system failed. They think *you* ignored them.

The common reaction is to declare a new rule from on high: "All requests must now go into this 37-field Jira form." This rarely works. People are creatures of habit. Sales will keep using Slack. Marketing will keep sending emails. A rigid, high-friction process just encourages them to find workarounds, and you’re back to the scavenger hunt.

### Stop Chasing. Create a Gravity Well.

You don’t need a more complex tool or a stricter policy. You need a simple, low-friction system that pulls information into one place. You need to create a gravity well for ideas, not a fortress with a single gate.

Here’s how to build one.

**1. Designate a Single, Lightweight Front Door**

Pick one place—and only one place—where official product input is captured. The tool itself is less important than your team’s consistency in using it.

It could be:
*   A dedicated Slack channel (e.g., `#product-intake`) with a pinned workflow that pops a simple form.
*   A basic Google Form or Airtable that anyone can fill out in 60 seconds.
*   A specific Jira project type with a simplified "New Idea" issue view.

The key is to make it ridiculously easy. If it takes more than a minute to submit an idea, people will default back to sending you a DM.

**2. Define the "Ticket to Ride"**

Your intake form shouldn’t be an interrogation. It just needs to capture the absolute minimum context required for a productive conversation. Ditch the fields for story points, t-shirt size, and epic links.

Start with these three questions:
*   **Problem:** What user or business problem are we trying to solve? (Frame it as a problem, not a solution like "add a blue button.")
*   **Audience:** Who is experiencing this problem? (e.g., "new users in their first week," "enterprise admins.")
*   **Evidence:** How do we know this is a real problem? (e.g., "a link to 5 Gong calls," "a screenshot from our analytics dashboard," "3 support tickets.")

This forces the requester to do a tiny bit of upfront thinking, which filters out low-effort suggestions and dramatically improves the quality of your backlog.

**3. Gently and Relentlessly Redirect**

This is the hardest and most critical step. For the next month, you must become a polite broken record.

When the CEO DMs you a feature idea, your response is:
> "This is fascinating. So we don't lose track of it, could you pop it into our product intake form? It will only take a minute and ensures it gets reviewed with the proper context. Here's the link: [link]"

When Sales emails you a request from a customer, your response is:
> "Thanks for flagging this! To make sure this gets into our triage process, please add it to our intake board. That's the single source of truth we use for prioritization. Here's the link: [link]"

You do this every single time. You aren’t being bureaucratic; you are being a good steward of everyone's ideas. You're guaranteeing their input won't get lost in your inbox. After a few weeks of consistent redirection, people will learn the new path. The behavior will change.

Your job is not to be an information archaeologist. It’s to understand the landscape of problems and make smart bets on what to solve next. A disciplined intake process stops the scavenger hunt and gives you back the time and mental space to do the real work.
