You asked the AI to “write user stories for the new dashboard.” You got back three paragraphs of generic nonsense that sounded like a marketing brochure. So you tried again: “be more specific.” The result was just as useless.

The problem isn't the AI. The problem is your prompt. You treated it like a search query, not a set of requirements.

When we write a product requirements document, we don’t just write “build a dashboard.” We provide context. We define the user. We specify the goals, the constraints, and the acceptance criteria. We do this because we know that ambiguity is the enemy of execution. Garbage in, garbage out isn't a new AI phenomenon; it’s a foundational principle of building anything.

Your AI prompt is a micro-PRD. Start treating it like one.

## The Vague Ask vs. The Clear Spec

Let’s look at a common scenario: generating competitive analysis.

**The Vague Ask:**
> `Summarize our competitor's pricing page.`

This prompt has no context. Who is the competitor? What are you trying to learn? Who is this summary for? The AI will give you a simple, literal summary that you could have written yourself in five minutes.

**The Product Spec Prompt:**
> `Act as a senior product marketing manager creating a competitive battlecard for our sales team. Our product is a B2B project management tool called "TaskFlow," which charges per seat. Our main competitor is "ProjectGrid."`
>
> `Analyze the pricing page at [URL] and create a summary in a markdown table with three columns: "Feature," "ProjectGrid's Approach," and "Our TaskFlow Advantage."`
>
> `Focus specifically on points that a non-technical salesperson can use in a discovery call. Omit details about their API tiers. The tone should be confident and direct.`

See the difference? One is a lazy question. The other is a well-defined task with all the necessary components for success.

## The 5 Elements of a High-Fidelity Prompt

A good prompt, like a good spec, provides clarity and direction. It doesn't have to be long, but it must be specific. Include these five elements to stop getting generic output.

### 1. Persona and Audience
Define who the AI should be and who it's creating the output for. This is the most overlooked and powerful component. It frames the entire response.

*   **Bad:** `Explain technical debt.`
*   **Good:** `Act as an engineering manager. Explain technical debt to a non-technical CEO, using a financial debt analogy. The goal is to secure a 10% budget allocation for refactoring in the next quarter.`

### 2. Context and Background
Give the AI the "why." What is the business situation? What problem are you trying to solve? Paste in relevant data, user feedback, or internal documents.

*   **Bad:** `Write an email announcing a feature delay.`
*   **Good:** `We are delaying the "Invoice Export" feature by two weeks due to a critical bug found in staging. Draft an internal email to the sales and support teams. They need to know the new launch date and the reason for the delay (a data integrity bug) so they can manage customer expectations. The tone should be transparent and accountable, not defensive.`
