It’s 9 AM on Monday. You filter your analytics dashboard for last week’s numbers, see a line going up and to the right, and take a screenshot. You paste it into the team Slack channel with a cheerful message: “Great work last week, team! Engagement is up 8%!”

You feel like you’ve done your job. You’ve communicated the results.

What you actually did was outsource your job to twenty other people. You threw a raw data point over the fence and made it everyone else’s problem to figure out what it means. You didn’t provide insight; you created a cognitive burden.

A screenshot is not a report. It’s a Rorschach test. The sales lead sees 8% and thinks, "Great, I can tell prospects the feature is a hit." The lead engineer sees 8% and thinks, "Is that because of my performance fix or the new feature? Was it a real increase or a tracking anomaly?" The marketing manager sees 8% and wonders, "Was that driven by our campaign, or is it organic?"

You, the product manager, are the single person who is supposed to have the most context. Dropping a chart without commentary is like a doctor handing a patient a blood test result without explaining what the numbers mean. It’s unhelpful at best and malpractice at worst.

Stop forwarding data. Start delivering diagnoses.

### The Three-Sentence Diagnosis

Next time you share a metric, don’t just post the chart. Add a simple, three-part annotation. Think of it as the narrative layer that turns data into intelligence.

1.  **The Observation:** State the fact. What does the chart literally say? Be specific.
    *   *Instead of:* "Engagement is up!"
    *   *Try:* "Weekly active users who completed a project increased by 8% week-over-week, from 4,100 to 4,428."

2.  **The Hypothesis:** Explain the *why*. This is where you connect the data to reality. It shows you’re thinking, not just observing. It’s okay if you’re not 100% certain, but you need a credible theory.
    *   *Instead of:* Leaving everyone to guess.
    *   *Try:* "We believe this is driven by the 'template suggestions' feature we launched last Monday, as the increase is concentrated among first-time users."

3.  **The Next Step:** What happens now? What should the team do with this information? This is the most critical part. It turns a passive update into an active plan.
    *   *Instead of:* Silence and confusion.
    *   *Try:* "We'll continue to monitor this cohort's retention. If the trend holds, we should prioritize adding more templates in the next cycle. No action is needed from the team right now."

### From Data Point to Decision Point

Let’s see it all together.

**The old way:**
*(Screenshot of a line chart going up)*
"Great work last week, team! Engagement is up 8%!"

**The new way:**
*(Screenshot of the same line chart)*
"**Observation:** Weekly active users who completed a project increased by 8% week-over-week, from 4,100 to 4,428.
**Hypothesis:** We believe this is driven by the 'template suggestions' feature we launched last Monday, as the increase is concentrated among first-time users.
**Next Step:** We'll continue to monitor this cohort's retention. If the trend holds, we should prioritize adding more templates in the next cycle. No action is needed from the team right now."

The second message takes you an extra 90 seconds to write. But it saves the rest of your team a collective hour of confused questioning, redundant analysis, and context-chasing. It preempts the inevitable "What do you think caused that?" and "What does this mean for our roadmap?"

This isn’t just about saving time. It’s about establishing your credibility. When you consistently provide a diagnosis, you show that you own the outcome, not just the report. You demonstrate that you understand the relationship between the work your team ships and the results the business sees.

Anyone can take a screenshot. A product manager provides the diagnosis.
