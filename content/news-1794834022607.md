The deploy went out at 4:47 on a Thursday. By 5:12 the error rate on the checkout service had tripled, and by 5:40 a staff engineer named Priya was staring at a Slack thread that read, in order: "rollback?" "rollback." "rolling back." What nobody typed, and what the incident channel would spend the next six hours carefully not saying, is that the rollback did not fix anything. It hid something. The bad commit was gone, the graphs went green, and the bug that caused it was still sitting in the codebase, now wearing a clean shirt and a plausible alibi.

This is the quiet scandal of modern software operations: the rollback has stopped being a safety mechanism and become a reputation-management tool. We deploy constantly, we revert instantly, and we have convinced ourselves that speed of reversal is the same thing as safety. It isn't. A rollback is a lid. What matters is whether anyone lifts it afterward.

## The arithmetic of hiding

Consider what a rollback actually does. It restores the previous binary. It does not restore the previous *understanding*. The commit that broke checkout is still in `main`, one `git revert` away from being re-applied by a well-meaning engineer who sees a clean diff and no annotation. The failing test that would have caught it was, in the rush, marked flaky and skipped. The dashboard that should have shown the error spike was tuned last quarter to reduce alert fatigue, and the threshold now sits just above the pain.

Every one of those is a small, defensible decision made by a reasonable person under time pressure. Stack twenty of them and you get an organization that can reverse a deploy in ninety seconds and cannot explain, three weeks later, why the same class of bug keeps shipping. The rollback didn't cause that. It *absorbed* the evidence.

There's a number that should worry engineering leaders more than MTTR, and almost none of them track it: the ratio of rollbacks to postmortems that name a root cause. In most teams I've seen, that ratio is embarrassing. Rollbacks are counted as incidents resolved. They are, functionally, incidents *deferred*.

## Why the lid stays on

The incentives are brutally simple. A rollback is a green checkmark. A root-cause investigation is a meeting where someone has to admit they shipped a bug, someone else has to admit their test was inadequate, and a third person has to admit the monitoring they built didn't catch it. Three reputations, one hour, zero visible output. The rollback asks for none of that. It asks you to press a button and go to bed.

So the culture optimizes for the button. Teams brag about deploy frequency and rollback speed the way a hospital might brag about how fast it discharges patients—impressive until you ask whether anyone got better. The postmortem, when it happens, becomes a ritual of blame-adjacent language: "human error," "insufficient testing," "monitoring gap." These are not causes. They are the places we stopped looking.

The tell is in the language of the incident channel. When a rollback resolves an outage and the thread ends with "nice work everyone," you have watched a cover-up in real time—not a malicious one, just a comfortable one. The bug is still there. The test is still skipped. The alert threshold is still wrong. The only thing that changed is that nobody is looking anymore.

## What lifting the lid costs

I'm not arguing against rollbacks. Reverting a bad deploy is correct and fast and saves customers real pain. The problem is treating the revert as the end of the story instead of the opening of an investigation. The teams that actually get better at this do something unfashionable: they require a rollback to *file a ticket against itself*. Not a postmortem template nobody reads—a specific, assigned, dated task: re-enable the skipped test, lower the alert threshold, add the missing assertion. The rollback closes the incident. The ticket keeps it open until the underlying defect is dead.

That sounds like bureaucracy. It is the opposite. It's the difference between a fire department that puts out fires and one that also investigates arson. The first is fast and busy forever. The second gets quieter over time.

The uncomfortable part is that this requires someone to own the ugly finding. The engineer who shipped the bug has to write it down. The person who skipped the test has to un-skip it. The manager has to defend the time spent on a defect that no longer hurts anyone—yet. That's a harder sell than a rollback button, and it's the only thing that breaks the cycle.

## The question nobody asks

Next time your team rolls back a deploy and the graphs go green, ask one question before the channel goes quiet: what did we just bury? If the answer is "nothing, we know exactly what happened and it's fixed," you're one of the rare teams doing this right. If the answer is a shrug and a "we'll look at it Monday," then Monday will arrive with the same bug wearing a new commit hash, and the rollback will be waiting to hide it again.

The deploy is fast. The revert is faster. The reckoning is the part we keep skipping—and it's the only part that compounds.
