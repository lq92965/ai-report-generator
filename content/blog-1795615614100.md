Three weeks into a platform migration, Dana sent the same 600-word status update to 34 people. The VP of Engineering replied in four minutes: "Too long, what do you need from me?" The finance analyst who only cared about the burn rate scrolled past. The support lead — the one person who could have flagged a customer-facing regression — didn't open it at all.

The update wasn't bad. The distribution was. Dana had built one message for one imagined reader, then sent it to everyone and hoped.

Most PM reporting advice focuses on what to write. This piece is about who you write it for, and how to split one project reality into three or four versions without tripling your workload. Call it a stakeholder map: a standing decision about who gets which slice of the truth, at what cadence, in what format.

## Start with decision rights, not org charts

The instinct is to sort stakeholders by seniority. Don't. Sort them by what they can actually decide.

Ask one question per person: *What is the next decision this person will make that touches my project?* If you can't name one, they probably belong on a broadcast list, not a tailored update.

A quick sort usually produces four buckets:

- **Deciders.** They approve scope, budget, headcount, or launch. They need options, tradeoffs, and a clear ask.
- **Blockers.** They can stop you — legal, security, a platform team whose API you depend on. They need timelines, dependencies, and early warning.
- **Doers.** Engineers, designers, analysts doing the work. They need specifics: what changed, what's next, what's stuck.
- **Watchers.** They want to know it's on track and nothing is on fire. They need a line or two, not a document.

The same person can sit in two buckets for different projects. That's fine. What matters is that you make the assignment explicit, once, and revisit it when the project phase changes.

## The four-line grid that replaces a 34-person email

Here's the practical move. For each bucket, define a fixed format and cadence in advance. Write them down. Then the weekly work becomes filling in blanks, not composing from scratch.

| Bucket | Format | Cadence | Length | Core content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deciders | Decision memo | Biweekly or at milestones | 1 page | Ask, options, recommendation, risk |
| Blockers | Dependency note | Weekly | 5 lines | Dates, interfaces, what we need from you |
| Doers | Working update | Weekly | Bulleted | Shipped, next, blocked, owner |
| Watchers | Broadcast line | Weekly or monthly | 2 sentences | RAG status + one headline |

You are not writing four reports. You are writing one report and cutting it four ways. The decision memo is the source of truth; the other three are extracts.

## Write the deciders' version first

This is the counterintuitive part. Most PMs write the team update first because it's easiest — everyone knows what shipped. But the decision memo forces you to articulate the actual choices in front of the project, and that clarity cascades down.

A decision memo has four fields:

1. **The ask.** One sentence. "Approve a two-week slip to add SSO before launch" — not "SSO status update."
2. **Options.** Two or three, with cost and consequence. Include "do nothing" if it's real.
3. **Recommendation.** Pick one. Say why in two sentences.
4. **What changes if you're wrong.** The reversibility check. If it's cheap to undo, say so; deciders relax when they know the exit cost.

If you can't fill in field one, you don't have a decision — you have a status update that will be read as noise. Send it to the watchers instead.

## Cut the same content for everyone else

Once the memo exists, the other three versions are mechanical.

**Dependency note for blockers:** strip everything except dates and interfaces. "Auth API contract frozen March 14. We need your staging keys by March 18 or the integration test slips a week." No narrative, no context. Blockers read for their own name and their own date.

**Working update for doers:** shipped / next / blocked, each with an owner and a link. Keep it under 150 words. If it's longer, the team has a coordination problem that a longer update won't fix.

**Broadcast line for watchers:** status color plus one sentence. "Green — on track for April 2 launch; SSO decision pending this week." That's it. Watchers who want more can ask; the point of the broadcast is to make silence meaningful.

## Set cadence by phase, not by habit

A project in discovery doesn't need a weekly decision memo — it needs a fortnightly one with a longer leash. A project in the last two weeks before launch might need a daily five-line dependency note.

Write the cadence into the stakeholder map itself, with a review trigger: "Revisit when we hit code freeze" or "Revisit if the RAG goes amber." Without a trigger, cadences calcify and you end up sending the same format long after the audience has changed.

## The one habit that keeps the map honest

Once a quarter, list every person receiving any version of your update and write one sentence next to each name: *the last time they acted on it.* If you can't remember, or the answer is "never," move them to the broadcast list or off it entirely.

This sounds harsh. It's actually a kindness. A 600-word email nobody reads trains people to ignore your name in their inbox. A two-line broadcast they actually scan keeps you on their radar for the moment it matters.

Dana's fix took one afternoon. She moved 22 people to a two-line broadcast, wrote a proper decision memo for the VP, and sent the support lead a five-line dependency note with the customer-facing risk called out. The next week, the support lead replied within an hour — with a regression Dana hadn't known about. The update didn't get better. The routing did.
