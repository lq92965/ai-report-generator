The email arrives at 3 a.m. A key vendor, the one whose API underpins a third of your product, has been acquired. The service will be sunsetted in 90 days. There is no migration path. The team that built it is already gone. Your engineers stare into their monitors, the hum of the servers in a faraway data center suddenly sounding like a landlord changing the locks. They have access, but they don't have control.

We traded the server room for the cloud, the upfront capital expenditure for a predictable monthly bill. We were told this was freedom. Freedom from racking hardware, from replacing failed drives, from managing cooling and power. It was a good trade, for a while. But we forgot what we gave up in the exchange: sovereignty. The master key. The root password.

Owning the hardware was a miserable job, but it came with one absolute guarantee. When you had physical access to the machine, you were god. You could wipe it, re-provision it, smash it with a hammer. No terms of service update could change the laws of physics. That box was yours. Your control was total, your ownership unambiguous.

Today, your infrastructure is a set of API calls and a credit card number. You have `sudo` privileges on a virtual machine, but that authority is a cheap counterfeit. It’s a pass granted by the real administrator, the platform owner, who can revoke it with a single line of code in their billing system. You are a tenant in a building you will never own, subject to the whims of a landlord you will never meet. The Broadcom acquisition of VMware wasn't a technical event; it was a reminder of this feudal arrangement. Customers who had built entire businesses on VMware's virtualization stack woke up to find their contracts voided, their pricing models exploded, and their licensing terms rewritten by an entity that saw them not as partners, but as assets to be squeezed.

This isn't just about price hikes. It’s about the silent, creeping risk embedded in every third-party dependency. The platform you build on can pivot. It can be bought. It can go out of business. It can decide you are no longer a customer it wishes to serve. Your root password is just a guest pass, and checkout time is non-negotiable. The digital foundations of your company are built on rented land, and the lease is always up for renewal.
