The call came from legal. A new sales rep, eager to close a Q3 deal, had used the company’s new internal chatbot to confirm discounting authority. "Can I offer a 30% discount for enterprise clients over 50,000 seats?" he’d asked. The bot, `InsightAI`, responded instantly: "Yes, discounts up to 35% are pre-approved for the Enterprise Growth Initiative." The rep sent the signed contract to finance.

The problem is, the Enterprise Growth Initiative was a failed proposal from 2018. The bot found the details in a PowerPoint deck saved on a long-forgotten marketing SharePoint site, created by a VP who left the company three years ago. The official, current discount cap is 15%. That contract is now a seven-figure mistake.

For twenty years, the corporate shared drive has been a digital landfill. It’s a sprawling mess of drafts, outdated policies, brainstorming notes, and redundant copies of files with names like `Final_Strategy_v4_Johns_edits_USE_THIS_ONE.pptx`. This was never a problem because a human was always the filter. We knew the institutional context. We knew which folders were graveyards and which documents were gospel. We navigated the mess with instinct and experience.

That era is over. In the rush to deploy Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, companies are pointing large language models at this entire, uncurated corpus. The AI has no instinct. It cannot distinguish between a final, board-approved policy document and a speculative draft. It treats a comment in a Google Doc with the same authority as a clause in a legal contract. The passive archive of corporate memory, once a place for storage, is now a live, programmatic resource. It has been made executable.

This is not a hypothetical risk about a bot saying something strange. It is a direct and present threat to core business operations. An AI assistant surfaces an old, more generous parental leave policy from an un-archived HR benefits draft, creating an instant employee relations crisis. A technical bot, trained on all of engineering’s documents, gives a junior developer instructions based on a deprecated security protocol, opening a massive vulnerability. The source of truth has been poisoned by its own history.

The first impulse is to launch a "data governance initiative." This is a fantasy. No organization has the time or manpower to manually sift through petabytes of accumulated digital history, tagging every file with the correct context, permissions, and expiration dates. It’s like trying to bail out the ocean with a teaspoon. The project will be obsolete before the first project plan is even approved.
