You’re in the quarterly planning meeting, walking through the roadmap. It’s going well until you get to the big one. “For Q3,” you say, “we need to prioritize refactoring the authentication service.”

You see the shift in the room. The Head of Sales leans forward. The VP of Marketing’s smile tightens. You try to explain. You use words like “brittle,” “scaling limitations,” and “developer velocity.”

They don’t hear any of that. What they hear is a six-week black hole where no new customer-facing features will appear. They hear that the competitor’s shiny new reporting tool will go unanswered. They hear that you, the product manager, are siding with engineering over the business.

The conversation is over before it begins. You’ve framed a critical investment as a cost center, and nobody buys cost centers.

## Stop Selling the Work. Start Selling the Outcome.

Technical debt is a product problem with an engineering solution. But we consistently pitch it like an engineering problem. We lead with the *how*—the code refactor, the database migration, the service decoupling.

Your stakeholders don’t care about the *how*. They care about the consequences. They operate in the world of revenue, risk, and customer commitments. Your pitch needs to live in that world, too. Talking about code health to a sales leader is like describing the chemical composition of a tire to someone with a flat. They just want to know when they can drive again.

You have to translate the abstract pain of engineering into the concrete pain of the business.

## Reframe Debt as Business Risk

The most effective way to get buy-in is to connect the tech debt to a specific, terrifying business outcome. Stop using vague terms and start using their language: money, customers, and reputation.

**Instead of saying:** “This old library is deprecated and has a lot of security vulnerabilities.”

**Try this:** “Our current payment processor library is no longer supported. This exposes us to a PCI compliance violation, which carries a potential fine of $500,000. It also means if we have a security breach, we could lose the trust of our enterprise customers.”

**Instead of saying:** “The user profile service is monolithic and hard to deploy.”

**Try this:** “Last quarter, we had three separate outages related to the user profile service, affecting 40% of our users at login. Each time a simple change was deployed, it took the whole system down. This is the part of the code that will prevent us from handling the holiday traffic surge, risking a major outage on our biggest sales day of the year.”

Suddenly, you’re not talking about boring infrastructure. You’re talking about risk mitigation. You’re the person protecting the company from disaster, not the one slowing down feature development.

## Connect Debt to Future Speed

Not all tech debt is a ticking time bomb. Sometimes, it’s a boat anchor. It doesn’t sink you today, but it makes every single journey slower and more exhausting. You need to quantify that drag.

Work with your engineering lead to gather metrics that illustrate the pain.

*   **Cycle Time:** “Teams working in the old billing codebase take three times longer to ship a feature than teams working in the new part of the app. Fixing this will let us launch monthly pricing tiers this year instead of next.”
*   **Onboarding Time:** “A new engineer takes six weeks to become productive in this part of the code, versus two weeks everywhere else. This slows down our entire hiring plan.”
*   **Bug Rate:** “Bugs originating from the checkout flow have increased 45% in six months. That’s one full-time engineer who is just fixing regressions instead of building the new features you’ve been asking for.”

This reframes the investment. You’re not stopping to fix something. You’re sharpening the saw. You’re making a down payment on future velocity, enabling the very features your stakeholders want—only faster and with higher quality.

## Make It a Line Item, Not a Debate

If you treat tech debt like a special, one-off project, it will always be debated against a revenue-generating feature. And it will almost always lose.

The best practice is to make it a non-negotiable part of your planning process. Work with engineering to allocate a fixed capacity—say, 20% of every sprint—to this class of work.

This approach does two things:
1.  **It makes the cost predictable.** It’s a “business tax” for running a healthy software company, just like paying for servers or software licenses.
2.  **It lowers the stakes.** You’re no longer asking for an entire quarter. You’re chipping away at the problem consistently, which prevents it from ever becoming a five-alarm fire that requires derailing the entire roadmap.

Your job as a product manager isn’t to be the voice of engineering in a business meeting. It’s to translate the needs of all parts of the organization into a single, coherent strategy. Stop explaining tech debt. Start explaining the business case for a stable, scalable, and speedy future.
