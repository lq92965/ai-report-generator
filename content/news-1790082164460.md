The product demo starts. A slick interface flashes on the screen, showing a complex logistics problem: a fleet of trucks, a dozen warehouses, and a storm system moving across the Midwest. The presenter types a plain-English query. "Given the weather and current inventory, generate the most efficient re-routing plan to minimize spoilage." A moment passes. Text streams into a box, outlining a new schedule with convincing, authoritative detail. Someone in the back of the room murmurs, "Wow, the reasoning engine is incredible."

That phrase is the tell. "Reasoning engine." It's the new magic word, the one that papers over a terrifying gap in understanding. We are not directing a machine; we are performing a ritual. We are islanders who saw a plane land once and now spend our days building bamboo fuselages and praying for cargo.

The rituals of this new cult are becoming standardized. There's the ceremonial "chain-of-thought" prompting, an incantation where we beg the model to "think step-by-step" as if we're coaxing a nervous student. There's the ritual of Retrieval-Augmented Generation, where we lay offerings of our own documents at the model's feet, hoping it will bless them with its attention. We tweak temperature settings like priests adjusting the flow of incense, seeking the perfect balance between rote dogma and creative madness. This isn't software engineering. It is high-tech divination.

The fundamental misunderstanding is this: a large language model is a sequence predictor. It is a brilliant mimic, an autocomplete on a cosmic scale. It arranges words in a statistically probable order based on the trillions of words it has already seen. It has no model of the world, no concept of cause and effect, no mechanism for verifying truth. A calculator *calculates* 5+3=8 because it understands the logic of arithmetic. An LLM says 5+3=8 because it has seen that string of text countless times in its training data. The difference is subtle. It is also the difference between a tool and an oracle.

And here are the stakes. When the cargo cult fails, the bamboo airplane doesn't just sit on the runway. It gets loaded with real cargo and pushed off a cliff. The "reasoning engine" tasked with optimizing a supply chain hallucinates a non-existent bridge, creating a plan that looks perfect on paper and sends a dozen trucks to the bottom of a river. The AI legal assistant, asked to "reason" about discovery documents, confidently summarizes a key contract but omits a novel, single-word clause that changes the entire meaning of the agreement, because it has never seen that pattern before. The failure mode is not a crash or an error code. The failure mode is a confident, plausible, and catastrophic lie.

We have been sold a miracle and we are desperate to believe in it. The pressure to integrate these "reasoning engines" into everything from medical diagnostics to financial auditing is immense. But we are building systems on a foundation of mimicry, not understanding. We are treating a world-class pattern-matcher as if it has achieved consciousness. The real work isn't in crafting cleverer prompts or building more elaborate ritual chains. The real work is in remembering that the machine doesn't think. It just predicts the next word. And we are the ones who have to live with the consequences when that prediction is wrong.
