You had the idea on a Tuesday morning. It was elegant, simple, and solved a real customer problem. Your first step was to get buy-in. Smart.

You booked a "listening tour." First, you met with the lead designer, who loved the concept but suggested adding a few more configuration options. Then came the engineering lead, who saw a path to implementation but wanted to fold in some tech debt cleanup. You walked sales through the mockups; they were thrilled but insisted it needed an enterprise-grade permissions model to close their biggest deal. Marketing wanted to make sure it had a "wow" moment for the next launch. Legal had a quick chat about data handling.

Everyone felt heard. Everyone felt included. You incorporated all the "small asks."

Now you’re in the big review. The elegant idea is gone. In its place is a Frankenstein's monster of a feature. It’s bloated, confusing, and serves ten different masters. The timeline has tripled. The clean user experience is a mess. The sales lead who got his permissions model is now complaining that the core feature is too complicated for a demo.

You aimed for alignment and ended up with a camel: a horse designed by a committee.

### You Confused Input with Consensus

Your mistake was treating every stakeholder suggestion as a requirement for buy-in. You tried to build consensus on the *solution*, which is a trap. When you give everyone a perceived veto, they will naturally solve for their own local optimum. Sales needs to hit their number. Engineering needs to maintain the codebase. Marketing needs a story to tell.

None of them are wrong. But none of them own the holistic customer experience. That’s your job.

Seeking consensus on a solution guarantees a watered-down result. Your goal is not to make every stakeholder happy with the final feature. Your goal is to make every stakeholder confident that you ran a sound process and made a well-reasoned decision.

### A Framework for Input Without Abdication

You need to gather intelligence from the smartest people in your company without letting them steer. This requires structure and clear expectations.
