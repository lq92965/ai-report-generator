You hit send. The message lands in the main company Slack channel, polished and concise. It’s the big one—the announcement for Project Chimera, the feature you’ve poured the last two months into. The little emojis start rolling in. A rocket ship. A party popper. A dozen thumbs-ups. You lean back, satisfied. Communication: complete.

A week later, a senior engineer DMs you. "Hey, just saw your note from last week. What exactly is changing for the mobile API? My team is a bit confused."

The satisfaction curdles. The emojis weren't comprehension. They were just polite acknowledgements. You achieved visibility, not alignment. Your broadcast into the void was mistaken for effective communication.

This is the trap of the general channel. It feels efficient to tell everyone at once, but you end up telling no one in particular. When you speak to everyone, you speak to no one. Here’s how to fix it.

## Ditch the Megaphone for a Handshake

A single, all-hands announcement assumes every reader needs the same information, at the same level of detail, with the same call to action. This is never true. Your head of sales doesn't need to know about the database schema change, and the backend engineering team doesn't need the updated sales script.

Stop broadcasting. Start narrowcasting.

Before you write a single word, map your audiences and their specific needs. For Project Chimera, it might look like this:

*   **Audience:** Core Engineering Team
    *   **What They Need:** Link to the technical spec, clear list of dependencies, and the deadline for code review.
    *   **Channel:** `#team-chimera-dev`

*   **Audience:** Sales & Customer Success
    *   **What They Need:** A one-pager on the new value proposition, key talking points, and the date they can start mentioning it to customers.
    *   **Channel:** `#sales-enablement`
