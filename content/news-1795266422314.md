The model card arrives as a PDF, eleven pages, attached to a vendor email with the subject line "Responsible AI Documentation — Confidential." It is the kind of document that gets forwarded to legal before anyone in engineering opens it. Page one lists intended uses. Page four lists "known limitations." Page nine is a table of benchmark scores, and page eleven is a paragraph, buried in 8-point type, that says the vendor "does not warrant fitness for any particular deployment context."

That last sentence is the whole story. Model cards were supposed to be the transparency layer of the AI boom — a nutrition label for a system nobody can fully inspect. Instead they have quietly become something else: a paper trail that shifts the risk from the company that trained the model to the company that deployed it. The card is not a disclosure. It is a receipt you sign by using the thing.

The mechanics are simple and, if you have ever bought a used car "as-is," familiar. A vendor ships a foundation model with a card that says it may hallucinate, may reproduce biases present in training data, may fail on long-context inputs, and should be evaluated by the deployer before production use. Every one of those sentences is true. Every one of them, in a lawsuit or a regulator's inquiry, becomes a line in the vendor's defense: we told them. The deployer, who has neither the compute nor the data to run a serious independent evaluation, is left holding a document that says "you were warned" in twelve different fonts.

The people writing these cards know. I talked to a model documentation lead at a mid-size AI lab who described the process as "writing the disclaimer first and the description second." Her team spends more time with legal than with the researchers who built the model. The card is reviewed by compliance, then by comms, and the version that ships is the version that survives both. "By the end," she said, "the most useful sentence is the one that protects us."

This is not a conspiracy. It is an incentive gradient, and everyone is sliding down it. Regulators asked for documentation, and they got it — hundreds of pages of it, in formats that differ from vendor to vendor, with no standard for what "evaluated" means. Deployment teams asked for transparency, and they got a document that is technically transparent in the way a terms-of-service agreement is technically readable. The European AI Act's transparency obligations have accelerated the trend: companies now produce model cards the way they produce privacy policies, as a compliance artifact whose primary function is to exist.

The real cost is not legal. It is epistemic. A model card that reads like a liability waiver trains the people who most need to understand a system to stop reading it. Engineers learn that the card is a formality, skim the intended-uses section, and move on. The genuinely useful information — which inputs the model was never trained on, where the evaluation set came from, what the failure modes looked like in practice — gets flattened into the same gray paragraph as the warranty language. The signal drowns in the disclaimer.

There is a version of this that works, and it is not exotic. It looks like a card that is short, specific, and honest about what was not tested. It names the evaluation datasets. It shows the failures, not just the scores. It says "we do not know how this behaves on X" instead of "deployers should evaluate for X." The difference is the difference between a nutrition label and a waiver you sign at the ski rental counter. One tells you what is in the food. The other tells you the mountain is not responsible if you break your leg.

The vendors will not volunteer that version, because the waiver version is cheaper and safer for them. The push has to come from the deployers, who are currently the ones absorbing the risk without the leverage to negotiate it. A procurement team that asks for the failure cases, not just the benchmark table, changes what gets written. A regulator that defines "adequate documentation" as "specific and falsifiable" changes what gets filed. Until then, the model card will keep doing what it does now: telling you, in eleven pages, that you are on your own.
