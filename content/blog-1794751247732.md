Tuesday, 9:40 a.m. Your VP drops a message in the channel: "Quick one — where are we on the migration?" You have the answer. You also have a status doc you updated Friday that says exactly this. She didn't read it. That's the real problem, and no amount of polishing the same document will fix it.

A progress report fails for one reason: the reader can't find the decision, the risk, or the ask in under thirty seconds. They skim, they miss it, they ping you. Your job is to make the ping unnecessary.

## The mistake almost everyone makes

Most progress reports are organized around what the team did. Tickets closed, meetings held, code shipped. That's a work log, not a report. Your reader — a director, a sponsor, a cross-functional partner — doesn't care what you did. They care whether the thing will land, what could stop it, and whether they need to act.

Flip the order. Lead with the answer, then the evidence. The activity belongs at the bottom, and only if someone asks.

## The structure that survives a skim

Here's the shell I use. It fits on one screen.

**Line 1 — Status and the one-sentence verdict.**
"On track. Auth service ships to staging Thursday; the only open risk is the vendor cert, and we have a fallback."

That's it. If the reader stops here, they know the state of the world.

**Line 2 — What changed since last report.**
Two or three bullets. Not everything that happened — only what moved the needle or changed the plan. "Cut scope on the CSV export to hit the date" belongs here. "Standup moved to 10:15" does not.

**Line 3 — Risks and blockers, with owners.**
One line each. Format: *Risk — impact — owner — date it needs resolution.* A risk with no owner is a wish. A risk with no date is background noise.

**Line 4 — Decisions needed.**
This is the section that earns the report its keep. If you need a call from your reader, say it plainly: "Need you to approve the extra contractor by Thursday or we slip a week." Vague asks get vague responses. Name the decision, the deadline, and the cost of not deciding.

**Line 5 — Next milestone and date.**
One sentence. Gives the reader a hook to check back against.

Everything else — the burn-down chart, the sprint summary, the list of completed tickets — goes below a divider marked "Details." Skimmers never reach it. Detail-oriented readers can.

## Write for the reader who has ninety seconds

A few habits that make the difference between a report that gets read and one that gets skimmed past:

- **Put the ask in the subject line.** If your report goes out as an email or a doc with a title, the title should carry the headline. "Migration on track; need contractor approval by Thu" beats "Weekly Status — Week 14."
- **Use numbers, not adjectives.** "Slightly behind" means nothing. "Two days behind the original date, recovering by Friday" means something.
- **Cut the hedging.** "We're hoping to," "it seems like," "potentially" — these words make a reader nervous. Say what you know and flag what you don't.
- **Name names.** "Someone needs to chase the cert" gets ignored. "Priya is chasing the cert, due Wednesday" gets done.

## The test before you hit send

Read your report as if you're the VP who sent that 9:40 a.m. message. Can you answer "where are we?" from the first line? Can you tell whether you need to do anything? If either answer is no, rewrite the top, not the bottom.

One more check: does every risk have an owner and a date? If not, you've written a worry list, not a report.

## Why this saves you time, not just theirs

The instinct is to write a longer, more thorough report so nobody has questions. In practice, longer reports get skimmed harder, and the questions come anyway. The five-line shell forces you to decide what actually matters before you write. That decision is the work. The writing is five minutes.

You'll still get the occasional "what's going on?" message. But when you do, you can reply with one line: "See the top of Friday's report — status is on track, the only risk is the cert, Priya owns it." After two or three of those, the messages stop.

The best progress report is the one that makes the follow-up question unnecessary. Write for the reader who has ninety seconds and a full inbox, and you'll both get your mornings back.
