It’s Tuesday morning. You hit send on the weekly performance report. You spent hours pulling data, crafting charts, and writing up careful, nuanced analysis. It’s a work of art. The Looker dashboard is linked, the data is clean, the insights are buried in there, waiting to be discovered.

You get a single Slack reaction: a thumbs-up emoji from your manager.

Then, in the Wednesday leadership meeting, the VP of Sales asks, "So, what's the latest on that checkout funnel drop-off? Are we looking into it?"

The answer was on page four of your report. The chart showing a 15% decline was on page three. The recommendation was on page five. They never got there. They scanned the first page, saw the usual top-line metrics looked fine, and moved on with their day.

Your report wasn't a report. It was a data graveyard.

## Stop Writing a Mystery Novel

We learn to write reports in school. We start with an introduction, build our case with supporting evidence, and save the grand conclusion for the final paragraph. We write like we’re building a legal argument, revealing the truth only after we’ve presented all the evidence.

This is exactly the wrong way to write for a busy stakeholder.

Your execs, your peers, and your cross-functional partners are not a captive audience. They are skimming their inbox between meetings. They give you 30 seconds, not 30 minutes. They aren't looking for a story; they are looking for a diagnosis and a prescription.
