The demo took ninety seconds. A procurement manager in Ohio uploaded a scanned invoice, watched an agent extract line items, cross-check them against a purchase order, and draft a dispute email. The vendor’s sales engineer smiled. Everyone in the room nodded. Then came the quiet part, delivered as a bullet on slide fourteen: to run this in production, the customer would need the vendor’s orchestration runtime, its managed vector store, its evaluation harness, and its audit dashboard. The agent wasn’t a product you bought. It was a tenant.

This is the shape of the current AI tooling market, and it deserves a blunter name than “platform lock-in.” The sandbox has become a company store. You can build anything you like inside it, as long as you buy every nail, every board, and every receipt from the same counter.

## The bill arrives in three envelopes

The first envelope is compute. Running a capable agent in a demo is cheap. Running it against real traffic is not. A single retrieval-augmented workflow can fan out into dozens of model calls per user request, each with its own token budget, retry logic, and latency ceiling. Vendors know this. That’s why the pricing page lists per-token rates that look reasonable until you multiply them by a realistic concurrency curve. The second envelope is storage: embeddings, conversation logs, evaluation datasets, and the “memory” layer that makes the agent feel coherent across sessions. The third is the one nobody budgets for — the observability and governance tier, sold as a separate SKU, priced per seat, and required before any serious enterprise buyer will sign.

Individually, each line item is defensible. Together, they form a toll structure. The customer isn’t buying software; they’re renting a storefront inside someone else’s mall, and the mall sets the hours, the signage, and the rent.

## Why teams walk in anyway

The honest answer is that the alternative is worse right now. Building an agent stack from open components means stitching together a model gateway, a vector database, a tracing layer, a prompt registry, and a policy engine — then hiring someone to keep them from drifting apart. That person is expensive and rare. The vendor’s bundle collapses six integration projects into one contract and one support line. For a team under pressure to ship something visible this quarter, the trade is rational.

The trap closes slowly. The first integration is a convenience. The second is a dependency. By the third, the team’s internal tooling assumes the vendor’s SDK, its auth model, and its data schema. Migrating later isn’t a refactor; it’s a re-platforming, with all the downtime and political cost that implies. The switching cost compounds quietly, buried in a hundred small assumptions that no one documented because no one planned to leave.

## The exit signs are painted over

Vendors will tell you the exit is easy. They’ll point to export tools, open standards, and “bring your own model” options. Look closer. Export gives you the data, not the behavior. The prompts, the retrieval strategy, the evaluation thresholds, and the guardrail logic stay behind, because they were never portable artifacts — they were configuration inside someone else’s runtime. An open standard that only one vendor implements faithfully is not a standard. It’s a brochure.

> The measure of a platform isn’t how fast you can get in. It’s how much of your work survives the day you decide to leave.

There’s a legitimate counterargument. Bundles create real efficiency, and premature portability is its own tax. A startup that hand-rolls every layer to preserve optionality may never ship. The point isn’t that bundling is wrong. It’s that the bundle is being priced and structured to make the exit cost invisible at signing and enormous at renewal.

## What a buyer should actually ask

Three questions cut through the slideware. First: if we fired you tomorrow, what would we still own, in runnable form, without your servers? Second: show us the migration runbook for a customer who left, with names and dates. Third: which parts of our agent’s behavior live in your system as configuration we cannot export? The answers will be uncomfortable, and that discomfort is the signal.

The company store always looked generous from the outside. It stocked everything, extended credit, and kept the lights on. It also kept the ledger, set the prices, and decided who could leave with what. The agent platforms now being sold to enterprises are building the same arrangement, one convenience at a time. The teams that survive the next renewal cycle will be the ones who read the ledger before they signed.
