It’s 6 PM. Your laptop is covered in fingerprints, your coffee is cold, and your calendar looks like a game of Tetris you lost. You were in back-to-back calls from 9:15 AM until now. You synced, aligned, touched base, and circled back.

You were busy. But were you effective?

How many of those meetings ended with a concrete, irreversible decision? How many problems were actually solved versus just… discussed? If you feel a pit in your stomach, you know the answer. You spent your day as a professional meeting attendee, not a product manager.

Your calendar isn't a passive record of your obligations. It's the most tangible artifact of your priorities. And a calendar full of aimless conversations is a sign that your priority is talking, not shipping.

The fix isn't "no-meeting Wednesdays" or more aggressive calendar blocking. The fix is to change the purpose of every meeting you accept.

### The "Sync" Is a Trap

The most dangerous meetings are the ones with vague, comfortable names. The "Weekly Sync." The "Project Touch-Base." The "Marketing & Product Catch-Up."

These meetings feel productive because people are talking. But they often lack the single most important ingredient: a stated, required decision. Without a decision to be made, a meeting is just a conversation. It’s a podcast you’re forced to be a guest on.

You drift from topic to topic. Someone gives a status update that could have been a Slack message. Someone else raises a risk without proposing a mitigation. You all agree to "take it offline" and sync up again next week.

You didn't make a decision. You just scheduled another meeting.

### Reframe Every Invite as a Question

From now on, treat every meeting as a forcing function for a decision. Before you accept an invite, ask the organizer (or yourself) one question: **"What is the key decision we need to make by the end of this hour?"**

If there isn't one, decline the meeting.

This simple question is a powerful filter. It forces clarity. It turns a vague "discussion" into a specific goal.

*   **Before:** "Let's sync on the Q3 roadmap."
*   **After:** "Do we agree to fund the 'International Expansion' theme or the 'Performance & Scalability' theme as our top priority for Q3?"

*   **Before:** "Design review for the new checkout page."
*   **After:** "Will we approve the single-page checkout design for development, or does it require another iteration to address payment options?"

An agenda should not be a list of topics. It should be a list of questions that will be answered. This makes the meeting's purpose brutally clear to every single person attending.
