# They're Arguing About Your Data, Not Your Recommendation

You spent three weeks building the model. You cleaned the joins, reconciled the duplicates, sanity-checked the outliers. You wrote the recommendation in plain language and put it on slide one. Then the meeting starts, and within ninety seconds someone is asking where the churn numbers came from. Twenty minutes later you're still defending a filter condition, and your recommendation never gets discussed.

This is the most common failure mode in analytical work, and it has almost nothing to do with the quality of your thinking. It has to do with the order in which you present it.

## The meeting is a trust exercise, not a logic exercise

Decision-makers don't evaluate recommendations in a vacuum. They evaluate them against their prior beliefs, and their prior beliefs are anchored to numbers they've already seen. If your dashboard says churn is 4.2 percent and the CFO remembers 3.1 percent from last quarter's board deck, the conversation is now about the gap, not your proposal.

You can be right and still lose the room. The recommendation is downstream of the data in their heads, whether you sequenced it that way or not.

## What actually triggers the argument

When stakeholders push back on your numbers, they're usually doing one of four things:

- **Checking whether the definition changed.** A metric that moved because you excluded trial accounts looks like a metric that moved because the business is failing.
- **Protecting a prior commitment.** If they already told the board a number, your number is a threat to their credibility, not just a data point.
- **Testing whether you'll fold.** Some stakeholders probe the methodology to see if the analysis is load-bearing or decorative.
- **Genuinely not understanding the pipeline.** They've been burned by a dashboard before and they don't trust the source.

Only the last one is a real data question. The other three are social, and no amount of methodology documentation fixes them.

## Pre-empt the fight before the meeting

The fix is unglamorous: front-load the reconciliation and make it boring.

**Send a one-page data memo 48 hours before the meeting.** Not the deck. A short document that states the source, the date range, the definitions, and any place your numbers diverge from what the audience has seen before. If churn went from 3.1 to 4.2, say so explicitly and explain the cause. Let them absorb it privately, where they have room to react without an audience.

**Name the disagreement in the memo.** If you know the sales lead thinks the pipeline is healthier than your model suggests, write that down. "This analysis assumes a 22 percent close rate; the sales team's working number is 28 percent. At 28 percent, the recommendation still holds but the timeline compresses by a quarter." Now the argument is pre-framed, and you've shown the conclusion is robust to the disagreement.

**Separate the data appendix from the recommendation.** Put the methodology in an appendix and the recommendation on slide one, but reference the appendix explicitly during the meeting. "The definitional changes are on page 14; I'll flag the two that matter." This signals you've done the work without making everyone sit through it.

## The one-question test

Before you present anything, ask yourself: if a skeptical stakeholder disagrees with my numbers, does my recommendation survive?

If yes, say so up front. "Even if you use the more conservative churn assumption, the conclusion is the same." That single sentence defuses most methodology fights because it removes the incentive to relitigate the data.

If no, you don't have a recommendation yet. You have a hypothesis that depends on a number nobody has agreed to. Go get agreement on the number first, in a smaller room, with the two or three people who will actually argue about it.

## Stop defending the pipeline in public

The meeting is the wrong venue for methodology debates. Methodology is a private conversation with the people who own the source systems. The public meeting is for decisions, and decisions require a shared factual base.

If you find yourself explaining a SQL join to a VP for the third time, the problem isn't your explanation. It's that you're having the wrong conversation in the wrong room at the wrong time.

**Takeaway:** Your recommendation will be judged by whether people trust your numbers, not by whether they follow your logic. Settle the data question before the meeting, in writing, with the people who will raise it. Then spend the meeting on the decision.
