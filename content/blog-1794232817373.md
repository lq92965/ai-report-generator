The Friday before launch, a VP forwarded my team's status report back to me with one line highlighted: "On track." Her note: "Your own doc says the payments integration is blocked. Which is it?"

She was right. I'd written "on track" out of habit, then buried a two-week dependency slip in paragraph four. The report was technically accurate and completely misleading. That's the failure mode of most project status reports — the status label and the body tell different stories, and nobody notices until the escalation.

Here's the template I've used since, built around a single rule: the status line must be defensible from the body, and the body must make the next decision obvious.

## The template

Copy this. It fits in a Slack message or a short email.

```
**Project:** [Name] — reporting period [dates]
**Status:** [GREEN / AMBER / RED] — [one sentence justifying the color]
**Done since last report:** [2–3 bullets, outcomes not activity]
**Next up (next 2 weeks):** [2–3 bullets with owner names]
**Blocked / at risk:** [what, who owns unblocking, by when]
**Decision needed from you:** [one specific ask, or "none this week"]
**Confidence in the date:** [High / Medium / Low] — [what would change it]
```

Seven lines. The two that do the real work are **Status** and **Confidence in the date**. Everything else is context for those.

## Why the RAG label needs a sentence attached

A bare color is a Rorschach test. Your "amber" might mean "we'll recover" and your sponsor reads it as "start writing the postmortem." I've watched a green/amber/red debate eat forty minutes of a steering meeting because nobody had defined the thresholds.

Two fixes:

1. **Define the colors once, in writing, at project kickoff.** My standard: green means scope, schedule, and budget are all within tolerance and no decision is needed. Amber means one dimension is off but we have a recovery plan with an owner and a date. Red means we need a decision or additional resources this week to hit the date.
2. **Attach the justification sentence every time.** "Amber — payments integration slipped two weeks; vendor committed to a fix by the 14th, which keeps launch inside the buffer." Now the color means something a reader can act on.

If you can't write the justification sentence without hedging, that's your real status. It's red or amber, and you've been rounding up.

## Write outcomes, not activity

The "Done" section is where status reports quietly die. Compare:

- *Activity:* "Worked on the API migration. Had several meetings with the platform team."
- *Outcome:* "API migration 60% complete — auth endpoints live in staging. Platform team signed off on the schema; no further design changes expected."

The first tells your reader you were busy. The second tells them where the project actually stands and what risk remains. Readers of status reports are scanning for deviation from plan — give them the delta, not the diary.

Same rule for "Next up." Every bullet gets an owner. "Finalize the pricing page" is a wish. "Finalize pricing page copy — Dana, by the 12th" is a commitment you can follow up on next week.

## The decision line is the point of the report

Most status reports end with a summary. Yours should end with an ask. If you genuinely need nothing, write "none this week" — that's a useful signal too, because it tells your sponsor they can skip the meeting.

When you do need something, make it a single sentence a reader could approve in a reply: "Need sign-off on the reduced scope for v1 (drop SSO to v1.1) by Wednesday so engineering can re-plan the sprint." Not "alignment on scope would be appreciated."

One ask per report. If you have four, you have a meeting, not a report.

## Set a cadence and defend it

Weekly works for most projects. Daily is noise unless you're in an incident. Monthly is too slow to catch a slip while it's still cheap to fix.

The cadence matters less than the consistency. Same day, same format, same length. Readers build a mental model of your reports, and the moment the structure changes they have to re-read instead of skim. I send mine Thursday afternoon — early enough that a decision request lands before the weekend, late enough to capture the week.

Keep a running archive in one place. When someone asks "when did we first flag the vendor risk," you want to point at the report from three weeks ago, not reconstruct it from memory.

## A filled-in example

```
**Project:** Checkout redesign — week of March 3–7
**Status:** AMBER — payments vendor fix slipped to March 14; recovery
plan holds the April 2 launch but removes the buffer.
**Done since last report:**
- New checkout flow live in staging for 80% of SKUs
- Load test passed at 2x peak traffic
- Legal approved updated terms copy
**Next up (next 2 weeks):**
- Re-run load test with payments fix — Priya, by March 17
- Final QA pass on mobile — Marcus, by March 21
- Cut launch comms draft — me, by March 19
**Blocked / at risk:** Payments vendor API fix delayed to March 14.
Priya owns daily check-ins; escalation path agreed if not shipped by the 14th.
**Decision needed from you:** Approve launching without the saved-cards
feature (moves to v1.1) so QA has a full week. Need by Wednesday.
**Confidence in the date:** Medium — holds if the vendor ships by the 14th;
slips one week if not.
```

Read that and you know exactly where the project stands, what's at risk, and what you're being asked to do. No meeting required.

## The habit that makes it work

Write the status line last. Draft the body first — what shipped, what slipped, what's next — then read it back and ask: what color does this actually describe? Writing the label first invites you to rationalize it. Writing it last forces it to match the evidence.

That one sequencing change is what would have saved me the forwarded email. The body knew the truth. The label just hadn't caught up.
