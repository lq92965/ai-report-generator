You’re in the sprint planning meeting. You’ve just finished presenting a clear, well-researched user problem: customers are struggling to find the "export to CSV" button on a critical dashboard. You expect a conversation about changing the button’s color, increasing its size, or moving it above the fold.

Instead, your lead engineer is already at the whiteboard. They aren't drawing a button. They are architecting a dynamic, configurable UI framework that would allow users to build their own dashboards. It’s an elegant, scalable, and technically fascinating solution to a problem you didn't know you had.

The team is energized. The technical challenge is exciting.

Your heart sinks. You came here to fill a pothole. They’re designing the Golden Gate Bridge.

## Why Your Small Fix Became a Mega-Project

This isn't a failure of engineering. It's a failure of framing. Engineers are builders and problem-solvers. When presented with a problem, they instinctively look for the most robust, comprehensive, and future-proof solution. Their job is to build systems that last.

If you describe a leaky faucet, they might see an opportunity to re-plumb the entire house with better pipes. It's not malicious; it's how they're wired. You pointed out a symptom, and they started solving for the root cause of all possible symptoms.

The mistake was giving them a destination without any guardrails. You defined the *what* (make exporting easier) but not the *how big*. To get the right-sized solution, you need to provide the right-sized constraints.

## Frame the Problem with Three Constraints

Your job isn't to dictate the technical solution. But it is your job to define the boundaries of the problem space. Before your team jumps to a solution, give them these three constraints.

### 1. The Time Constraint (The "Timebox")

This is the most direct way to anchor the scope. Don't let the effort be an output of the solution; make it an input. This forces a conversation about trade-offs from the very beginning.

*   **Instead of:** “We need to make this easier for users.”
*   **Try:** “How could we make this 20% easier for users in the next sprint?”
*   **Or:** “What’s the most impactful improvement we can ship for this with less than 8 story points of effort?”

By fencing the problem with time or effort, you force creativity toward efficiency. The question shifts from "What is the best possible solution?" to "What is the best solution possible within these limits?"

### 2. The Impact Constraint (The "Good Enough" Bar)

What does success actually look like? If you don't define the finish line, your team will run forever. Tie the solution to a measurable outcome.

*   **Instead of:** “Users are confused by the checkout flow.”
*   **Try:** “We’ve seen a 15% drop-off rate on the payment page. A successful solution will get that drop-off rate below 10%.”
*   **Or:** “Success for this project means a 50% reduction in support tickets related to ‘can’t find my invoice.’”

This anchors the discussion in user value, not technical purity. It gives the team a clear target and helps them know when the job is done. Once the metric is hit, you can stop. The pothole is filled.

### 3. The System Constraint (The "Blast Radius")

A small change in one part of the product can have cascading effects elsewhere. Engineers rightly worry about this, which can lead them to propose larger, systemic changes to avoid creating tech debt. You can preempt this by defining the acceptable "blast radius" for the solution.

*   **Instead of:** “Let’s add a search filter to this list.”
*   **Try:** “Let’s add a search filter here, but the solution can’t require any changes to the database schema.”
*   **Or:** “We need to solve this within the existing front-end component library. A good solution won't require building a new component.”

This doesn't mean you ignore tech debt. It means you make a conscious choice about when to take it on. Defining the blast radius keeps the team focused on the immediate problem and prevents a targeted fix from turning into an unplanned refactor.

## A Tale of Two Problem Statements

Let's see it in action.

**The "Suspension Bridge" Framing:**
> “Users can’t find the export button. We're getting a lot of complaints, and it's a big pain point. We need to make it more prominent.”

This is a vacuum. It invites the biggest, most elegant thinking.

**The "Pothole Patch" Framing:**
> “This month, we’ve seen a 40% increase in support tickets about users not finding the export button on the main dashboard. Our goal is to **reduce those tickets by half within the next sprint.** We need a solution that **doesn’t require changing the global navigation bar** and can be implemented by **one engineer in under a week.** What are our options?”

This is a well-defined box. It's specific. It's measurable. It's constrained. It channels your team's massive brainpower into finding the sharpest, fastest, most effective solution that fits inside that box.

You're not stifling creativity. You're focusing it. By providing clear constraints, you empower your engineering team to solve the right problem at the right size. Don't just show them the pothole; tell them how big the patch needs to be.
