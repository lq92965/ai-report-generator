Most project problems don't announce themselves. They sit in a Slack thread nobody replied to, a Jira ticket that's been "in progress" for eleven days, or a dependency the team keeps mentioning in passing. By the time these show up in a status report, they're already expensive.

The fix isn't a heavier reporting process. It's a short, structured scan you run once a week — before you write anything — so the report reflects what's actually happening instead of what everyone assumed last Monday.

Here's the scan I use. It takes about five minutes once you've done it a few times, and it catches the drift that status meetings are too polite to surface.

## Why a scan, not a meeting

Status meetings collect what people remember. A scan collects what the system shows. Those are different data sets, and the gap between them is where surprises live.

The scan also changes your posture. Instead of asking "how's it going?" and accepting "fine," you arrive at the meeting with three specific things to check. That's a much shorter conversation, and it's harder to wave off.

## The five checks

Run these in order. Keep a scratch note open — you're collecting evidence, not writing prose yet.

### 1. The silent-ticket check (60 seconds)

Filter your tracker for items that have changed status zero times in the last seven days but are still marked active. Not blocked — just quiet.

Quiet isn't automatically bad. Some work is genuinely heads-down. But a ticket that's been "in progress" through two status cycles with no comment, no commit, and no sub-task movement is a question worth asking directly: "Is this still the right size, or did it turn out bigger than we thought?"

You're not auditing anyone. You're looking for the work that's quietly gotten harder without anyone saying so.

### 2. The unanswered-question check (60 seconds)

Search your team channels for questions that got a reply from someone other than the person who needed to answer, or no reply at all. The classic pattern: an engineer asks the designer a question, a teammate says "good question," and it dies there.

Every one of these is a latent blocker. List them. In the meeting, you're not re-asking the question — you're naming the person who owns the answer and asking when they'll have it.

### 3. The dependency check (60 seconds)

Pull up the two or three external dependencies your project can't ship without. For each one, answer: what's the most recent evidence that it's moving?

"Evidence" means a merged PR, a signed doc, a scheduled date, a demo — something with a timestamp. "They said it's on track" is not evidence. If the last real signal is more than a week old, that dependency goes on your watch list, and you say so in the report.

### 4. The scope-creep check (60 seconds)

Compare this week's active work against the scope you agreed to at the start of the quarter or the project. Not to police anyone — to notice.

New items that appeared without a corresponding trade-off are the single most common reason projects slip while every individual status update says "on track." If three things got added and nothing got removed, the math doesn't work, and your report should say that plainly.

### 5. The "what would surprise me" check (60 seconds)

Close your eyes for a second and ask: if this project went sideways in the next two weeks, what would be the cause?

Whatever comes to mind first — write it down. Then check whether your current report mentions it. If it doesn't, that's your headline. The thing you'd be embarrassed to have missed is almost always the thing worth naming early, while it's still cheap to fix.

## Turning the scan into a report

The scan produces raw material. The report turns it into a decision for the reader.

A useful pattern: lead with the one thing that changed this week, follow with the one thing you need from the reader, and close with the watch list from your scan. Three short sections, no more.

For example:

> **Changed:** Payments integration moved from "on track" to "at risk" — the vendor's sandbox has been down since Tuesday and we've lost three test days.
>
> **Need:** A decision by Thursday on whether to slip the beta by a week or ship without the refund flow.
>
> **Watching:** The auth dependency hasn't produced a signal in nine days; I'm asking for a demo Friday.

That's it. The reader knows what moved, what they owe you, and what you're tracking. No paragraph about how busy everyone is.

## The failure mode to avoid

The scan goes wrong when it turns into surveillance. If your team senses you're hunting for people who didn't update their tickets, they'll update their tickets performatively and the signal dies.

Keep the framing on the work, not the person. "This ticket hasn't moved — is it bigger than we thought?" lands very differently from "why hasn't this moved?" The first invites information. The second invites defensiveness, and defensiveness is where status reports go to die.

The scan is for you. The report is for the reader. Keep those two jobs separate and both get easier.

## A note on tooling

You can run this entirely by hand in a tracker and a chat app. If you want to speed it up, a saved filter for "active, no updates in 7 days" and a search for unanswered mentions will do most of the work. Resist the urge to automate the fifth check — "what would surprise me" is a judgment call, and it's the one that catches the things filters can't see.

Five minutes, five checks, one honest report. That's the whole method. The teams that look calm in a crisis usually aren't luckier than yours — they just found the problems a week earlier, when they were still small enough to fix in a conversation.
