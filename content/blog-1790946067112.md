The kickoff meeting was going smoothly. You pasted the AI-generated Product Requirements Document into Confluence, and it looked perfect. The sections were clean, the user stories were well-formed, and the language was crisp. You felt efficient.

Then, your lead engineer, Maria, unmuted. "On page three, requirement 4.2 says the caching layer must have a TTL of 60 seconds. Can you walk me through the trade-off analysis for that number versus, say, 5 minutes? I'm worried about stampeding herd effects on a cold cache."

Silence.

You stared at the sentence. It sounded authoritative. It sounded correct. But you have no idea where the number 60 came from. The AI put it there. You outsourced the writing, but you accidentally outsourced the thinking, too. Now your credibility is on the line.

Generative AI is an incredible tool for overcoming the blank page. But it’s a terrible replacement for product thinking. It creates a convincing veneer of understanding, a document that looks like a PRD but crumbles under the slightest pressure. Your job isn't to produce the artifact; it's to own the logic that underpins it.

When you hand off a spec, you're not just handing off words. You're handing off a set of decisions, trade-offs, and hypotheses. If you can't defend them, the document is worthless. Here’s how to use AI to build your PRD without abdicating your responsibility.

### Use AI for Scaffolding, Not Architecture

Stop asking the AI to "write a PRD for X." You are the architect; the AI is the intern who can quickly frame a few walls. Your job is to provide the blueprint.

Instead of a single, lazy prompt, treat it as a conversation. Feed it the raw materials—your messy notes, user interview quotes, market data—and give it specific tasks.

*   **Good prompt:** "Here are five user quotes about our confusing checkout process. Synthesize these into three distinct problem statements."
*   **Good prompt:** "Based on this problem statement, generate five potential user stories. Frame them using the 'As a..., I want..., so that...' format."
*   **Bad prompt:** "Write a PRD for a better checkout."

The AI can structure your thoughts and translate them into standard formats. It can’t supply the core insights. You are still the one connecting the user's problem to the team's solution.

### Prompt for Questions, Not Just Answers

The most dangerous part of an AI-generated doc is the unexamined assumption. The AI will confidently state a requirement without knowing the context, the technical constraints, or the business goals. You need to uncover those assumptions before your engineers do.

Use the AI to pressure-test your own thinking. Turn it into a virtual "Red Team" that helps you find the weak spots in your plan.

*   **Instead of:** "Write the requirements for a new login page."
*   **Try:** "What are the 10 most critical security and data privacy questions an engineering lead would ask about a new login page?"
*   **Or:** "List the top five edge cases for a user trying to reset their password. What could go wrong?"

This flips the script. The AI’s output is no longer a finished product to be copied and pasted. It’s a checklist of questions you now have to answer. You’re using it to deepen your own understanding, not just to generate text.

### Own the "Why" Behind Every Sentence

Once you have a draft, your real work begins. Go through it line by line and act as the most skeptical stakeholder you can imagine. For every requirement, every user story, every acceptance criterion, ask yourself two questions:

1.  Can I explain *why* this is here?
2.  Can I trace it back to a specific user insight, data point, or business objective?

If the AI wrote, "The dashboard must provide a real-time view of user activity," you need to be able to translate that. Your version, backed by real thinking, sounds like this: "Our support team gets 20 tickets a day asking if a customer's file has finished processing. A real-time view will deflect these tickets and maps directly to our goal of reducing support costs by 15% this quarter."

One is a generic statement. The other is a defensible decision.

An AI can help you write more and faster. But it can’t build your credibility or earn your team’s trust. That comes from having done the hard work of thinking, questioning, and connecting the dots. The next time an engineer asks you "why," make sure the answer comes from you, not from a language model.
