It started in Slack. A message from the head of sales pops up. “Hey, quick one. Can we just add a ‘Source’ field to the new lead form? Should be super simple. Thanks!”

You want to be helpful. You want to be a team player. It *does* sound simple. So you reply, “Sure, let me check with the team.” You drop it into the engineering channel, feeling efficient.

An hour later, your tech lead messages you back. “About that ‘simple’ field… it touches the CRM integration, the marketing automation pipeline, and the reporting database. It’s not on the user object, so we’d have to change the schema. We’ll also need to update the API documentation and get security to sign off. This isn’t a small tweak. This is a project.”

The sprint plan is now a work of fiction.

## The Anatomy of a 'Simple' Request

We’ve all been there. A stakeholder, with the best of intentions, identifies a seemingly trivial change. But what they see as a pebble tossed in a pond, you know can create a tsunami in the codebase.

These "small tweaks" are product management landmines. They rarely account for the hidden costs:

*   **Discovery Cost:** What does it *actually* take to implement this? Your engineer just spent an hour figuring that out instead of working on the sprint goal.
*   **Dependency Cost:** What other systems, teams, or workflows does this touch? Sales saw a form field; your engineer saw three downstream integrations that would break.
*   **Design & UX Cost:** Does this new element fit the existing design system? Does it clutter the interface? Does it introduce a weird step into a user flow we just spent months simplifying?
*   **QA & Testing Cost:** A new field needs to be tested. It needs to be tested on different browsers, with different inputs, and across the entire user journey it impacts. The QA scope just expanded.
*   **Opportunity Cost:** This is the big one. Every cycle spent on an unplanned tweak is a cycle *not* spent on the strategic priority your team committed to.

Agreeing to a "small tweak" on the fly isn't being agile. It's setting your team up to fail.

## Your Playbook for Handling Drive-By Requests

Your job isn’t to say “no.” It’s to expose the true cost of the request and force a trade-off conversation. You can’t do that in a quick Slack reply. You need a system.

### Step 1: Acknowledge and Defer

Never give an answer on the spot. Your immediate goal is to make the stakeholder feel heard while buying yourself time to investigate.

**Don’t say:** “No, we’re busy.”
**Don’t say:** “Sure, seems easy.”

**Instead, say:** “Thanks for flagging this. I can see why that would be useful. Let me sync with engineering to understand the full scope, and I’ll get back to you by tomorrow with what it would take.”

You’ve validated their idea without committing to anything. You’ve set a clear expectation for your next step.

### Step 2: Triage with Your Tech Lead

Take the request to your engineering partner. This isn’t a full-blown planning session. It’s a 10-minute huddle to get a gut check.

Ask two questions:
1.  “On a scale of a t-shirt (S, M, L, XL), how big is this?”
2.  “What are the non-obvious risks or dependencies we should know about?”

This gives you enough information to understand the true size of the ask. An “S” might be something you can actually fit in. An “L” is a clear signal that this needs to be prioritized against other initiatives.

### Step 3: Reframe the Conversation Around Trade-Offs

Now, circle back to the stakeholder. Armed with the true cost, you can change the nature of the discussion. You’re no longer debating the merit of their idea; you’re discussing its priority relative to everything else.

**Don’t say:** “Engineering said this is too hard.”

**Instead, say:** “I have an update on the ‘Source’ field. We’d love to get it in for you. The team estimates it as a medium-sized effort, likely taking one engineer about four days. To fit it into this sprint, we would need to push out the work on the dashboard filtering feature. Given our Q3 goals, which of these delivers more value right now?”

You’ve turned a yes/no question into a strategic choice. You’ve made the trade-off visible. More often than not, when faced with a clear choice, the stakeholder will realize their "small tweak" can wait.

## Stop Being a Go-Between, Start Building a System

Handling these requests individually is exhausting. The real solution is to create a clear, visible intake process for all new ideas, no matter how small.

This doesn't need to be a bureaucratic nightmare. It can be as simple as:
*   A dedicated Slack channel (`#product-ideas`) where all requests must be posted.
*   A simple form that asks for the problem, the proposed solution, and the business impact.
*   A "Backlog" or "Triage" column on your public-facing project board.

The tool doesn't matter. The principle does: get requests out of DMs and into a centralized place where they can be seen, discussed, and prioritized against everything else.

Your role isn't to be a concierge for every idea. It's to be the ruthless guardian of your team's focus. The next time you get a request for a "small tweak," don't just answer the question. Use it as an opportunity to clarify the true cost of the work and protect the promises you've already made.
