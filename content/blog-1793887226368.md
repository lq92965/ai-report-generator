The report you write on Friday gets read by someone who wasn't in the room on Tuesday, on a team that will be restructured in six weeks. Most weekly status reports assume a stable audience and a fixed org chart. Neither holds. The ones that keep working are written for a reader who arrives cold, months later, with no memory of the context you had in your head when you typed it.

This is a guide to writing that report. Not the one that satisfies a template. The one that still makes sense when the reader is a stranger.

## Write for the reader who joins in March

Before you type a single line, picture the person reading it: a new hire on the team, a skip-level who inherited your group, or you, pulling up Q1 to answer a question in Q3. None of them share your context. All of them need three things fast: what changed, what's stuck, and what you need from them.

A status report that opens with "Continued work on the migration" tells that reader nothing. A report that opens with "Migration is 60% done; the last two services are blocked on a security review that's been open 11 days" gives them a decision to make.

The test is simple. Hand your draft to someone who missed the week. If they can't tell you what moved, what didn't, and what needs a decision in under 60 seconds, rewrite the top.

## The three-block structure that holds up

Every weekly report I've seen survive a reorg uses the same skeleton, regardless of template:

1. **Headline** — one sentence on the state of the work. Where are we against the goal?
2. **What moved** — two to five bullets on things that actually changed this week. Not activities. Changes.
3. **What's stuck and what I need** — the asks, the blockers, the decisions you're waiting on.

Everything else — metrics tables, links to docs, milestone dates — is supporting material. It goes below the fold. The reader who wants it will scroll. The reader who doesn't needs the top three blocks to be self-contained.

This is why the "activities" version of a status report fails. "Attended planning, reviewed three PRDs, synced with design" is a timesheet, not a status. Nobody downstream can act on it.

## Write the headline last, but put it first

The single highest-leverage line in your report is the headline. Write it after you've drafted the body, when you know what actually happened, then move it to the top.

A weak headline: "Week 12 update — payments workstream."

A working headline: "Payments is on track for the March 15 launch, but the fraud-check integration slipped a week and will land March 8."

The second version tells the reader the answer to the question they came to ask, includes a risk, and gives them a date to check back against. It also makes the rest of the report optional for anyone who only needed the headline.

If you can't write a headline that contains a status word — on track, at risk, off track, blocked — you don't yet know the status. Go find out before you write the report.

## Name the ask, and name the person

Blockers written as "waiting on legal" are dead ends. Blockers written as "waiting on Priya in legal for the DPA redline; asked Tuesday, needed by Thursday for the March 15 date" get resolved.

The difference is specificity on three axes: who, what, and by when. A blocker without all three is a complaint, not an ask. If you can't name the person, name the role and say you don't know who owns it — that's still actionable, because the reader can route it.

One more rule: put asks in the first screen. Buried asks don't get answered. If your report has a "requests" section at the bottom, move it up. The people who can unblock you read the top and skim the rest.

## Show the delta, not the state

A weekly report is a diff, not a snapshot. The reader already knows what existed last week. What they don't know is what changed.

Compare:

- "Q2 roadmap: 14 items, 6 in progress, 2 done."
- "Q2 roadmap: shipped the SSO integration (was in progress last week); pulled the analytics dashboard out of Q2 because design capacity is consumed by the mobile revamp."

The second version tells the reader what to update in their head. The first makes them do arithmetic.

If you track a metric, show the number and the change. "Weekly active users: 12,400 (up 3% from 12,040)." A number without a delta is a data point looking for a question.

## Keep a running log so Friday takes 10 minutes

The hardest part of a weekly report isn't writing it. It's remembering what happened. If you're reconstructing the week from memory on Friday afternoon, you'll miss things and over-weight whatever happened most recently.

Keep a scratch file open all week. Every time something changes — a decision, a blocker clears, a date slips — drop a one-line note with the date. On Friday, you're editing, not writing. The report takes ten minutes instead of forty, and it's more accurate.

This is also the cheapest way to survive a reorg. When your team gets restructured and a new manager asks "what's the state of X," you have six months of dated notes to pull from instead of a blank page.

## AI can draft the bullets. It can't know what mattered.

Language models are good at turning your scratch notes into clean sentences. They are bad at knowing which of the twelve things that happened this week actually mattered to the reader. If you hand the model your notes and ask for a status report, you'll get a tidy summary that treats every item as equally important — which is the same failure mode as the timesheet version.

Use the model for the mechanical parts. Feed it your notes and ask for a first pass on the "what moved" bullets. Then edit hard: cut the ones that didn't change anything, sharpen the ones that did, and write the headline yourself. The headline is the part that requires judgment about what the reader needs, and that's the part you shouldn't hand off. (For a fuller breakdown of which PM tasks to hand to a model and which to keep, the decision guide on AI tools for product managers is worth reading alongside this one.)

One concrete workflow that works: paste last week's report and this week's notes into the model, and ask it to list only the differences. That gives you a delta to edit, which is closer to what you actually need than a from-scratch draft.

## Three small habits that compound

**Send it at the same time every week.** Predictability is a feature. Readers learn when to expect it and stop asking.

**Keep the format identical week to week.** If the headline is always the first line and the asks are always the third block, readers can navigate by position. Changing the structure every week forces them to re-read.

**Archive the reports in one place with searchable dates.** The report's second life is as a record. Six months from now, someone will ask "when did we decide to cut the analytics dashboard?" The answer should be findable in ten seconds, not reconstructed from Slack.

## The version that survives the reorg

A weekly status report earns its keep when the reader is not the person you wrote it for. Write the headline for the stranger. Put the asks where they'll be seen. Show the delta. Keep notes all week so Friday is easy. Hand the drafting to a model and keep the judgment for yourself.

Do that, and when the reorg lands and your new skip-level opens your report cold, they'll know exactly where things stand in the first sentence. That's the report that survives.
