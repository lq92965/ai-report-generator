The graph on slide four was perfect. A clean, upward-sloping line showing a 30% increase in "user engagement." The execs nodded. The team felt a rush of success. You’d shipped the redesign, and the numbers proved it was working.

Two months later, your finance partner flags a problem. Churn is up. A small but meaningful number of your highest-value customers have quietly canceled their subscriptions.

You dig in. That beautiful "user engagement" metric you tracked was *time on page*. The redesign, it turns out, made a critical workflow so confusing that users were spending more time just trying to figure it out. They were more "engaged," but they were furious. The data was correct—time on page *did* go up. But the conclusion you drew from it was dead wrong.

This is the proxy trap. We measure what is easy to measure, not what is important. We trust the simple number on the dashboard and use it to make a decision that feels precise, data-driven, and completely defensible. But it's a decision built on a foundation of sand.

### The Averages Are Lying to You

Aggregate data is the enemy of good product decisions. It smooths out the jagged edges of reality into a comforting, simple line.

Your "average session duration" is a classic example. An average of 3 minutes might look healthy. But it could be hiding a brutal truth: 80% of your users bounce in 10 seconds, while a small group of power users stays for 15 minutes. The average tells you nothing about the experience of the vast majority. Making a decision based on that 3-minute average is like designing a product for a user who doesn't exist.

The problem isn't that the data is bad. The problem is that it's incomplete. It answers "what happened" but gives you zero clues about "why" or "to whom."

### Treat Your Analytics Spec Like a Product Spec

We spend weeks refining a PRD but treat instrumentation as an afterthought. "Hey, can you add an event when the user clicks this?" we ask the engineer, hoping for the best.

This is how you end up with flawed data. An event is fired, but it’s missing the `user_id` property. Or it tracks the click but not which variant of the button they saw. You get a number, but it lacks the context needed to be useful. Your data becomes directionally correct at best.

Your analytics implementation deserves the same rigor as your feature development.

**1. Write an Analytics Spec.**
Before a single line of code is written, document the "why" behind your measurement.
- **Hypothesis:** What do you believe will happen? (e.g., "We believe adding a setup wizard will increase successful onboarding.")
- **Key Metric:** What specific number will prove or disprove this? (e.g., "Increase the percentage of new users who complete all 3 setup steps from 40% to 60%.")
- **Instrumentation Plan:** What exact events and properties are needed?
    - `event: setup_wizard_started` (properties: `user_id`, `plan_type`)
    - `event: setup_wizard_step_completed` (properties: `user_id`, `step_number`)
    - `event: setup_wizard_finished` (properties: `user_id`, `time_to_complete_seconds`)

**2. Test Your Tracking in Staging.**
Don't wait until the feature is in production to see if the data is flowing correctly. Have your QA team, or do it yourself: trigger the events in a development environment. Open up your analytics tool and look at the raw event stream. Does it match your spec? Are all the properties there? Is the `user_id` a real ID or is it `null`? Find and fix these issues before they pollute your dataset forever.

**3. Run a Metric Pre-Mortem.**
Get your team in a room and ask one question: "If this metric goes up, what bad thing could also be happening?"
- If we increase "invites sent," are we just encouraging users to spam their contacts?
- If we increase "photos uploaded," are we seeing a rise in low-quality or policy-violating content?
- If we increase "time on page," did we just make the product harder to use?

This exercise forces you to identify the potential negative consequences and define counter-metrics. You might decide to track "invites accepted" alongside "invites sent," giving you a much healthier view of the feature's impact.

A dashboard gives you a summary. Your job is to challenge it. Go a layer deeper. Segment by user type, by platform, by tenure. Look for the cohorts who are having a terrible experience, even if the average looks fine. The truth isn't in the clean, upward-sloping line. It's in the messy, contradictory details that line is hiding.
