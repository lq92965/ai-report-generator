How to Write a Sales Report That Your Manager Actually Reads

Reportify AI Editorial
Staff Writer

A sales report is a snapshot of how much revenue you created, where it came from, and what is likely to close next. It is one of the few documents that directly connects your daily work to the company's bank account. Write it well and you give leadership a clear picture of pipeline health. Write it poorly and you bury the one number they care about under a pile of charts nobody asked for.

This guide walks through how to write a sales report that is short, honest, and useful: what to put inside, a template you can copy, a real example, and the mistakes that make sales reports useless.

Why Sales Reports Matter

Sales is the function every business judges by results. A good report does three jobs at once. It shows what happened, it explains why, and it tells the reader what to expect. When you report on a regular cadence, your manager stops asking "how are things going" in meetings because the answer is already on the page.

A consistent report also protects you. If a quarter comes in light, the report from two weeks earlier should already show the pipeline thinning. That turns a surprise into a managed expectation. People trust reps who flag problems early far more than reps who only deliver good news.

Weekly vs Monthly: Pick Your Cadence

The cadence decides how much detail each report carries. A weekly sales report stays tight: revenue, pipeline movement, and the one or two deals that changed. You write it in ten minutes because the window is short and the memory is fresh. A monthly report widens the lens. It adds trends, segment breakdowns, and a longer forecast, because a month is long enough to show direction, not just motion.

If you sell with a short cycle — say, transactional software or retail — report weekly so small slips get caught early. If your deals take a quarter to close, a monthly report with a strong pipeline view does the job, and a weekly note becomes noise. Most teams run both: a light weekly pulse and a heavier monthly review. The template in this guide serves both; only the depth of the forecast and the segment lines change.

What Goes in a Sales Report

A sales report needs to answer four questions: how much did we sell, against what target, what changed since last period, and what is the forecast. Everything else is supporting detail.

Include these sections:

  • Period and owner — the date range covered and who is reporting.
  • Revenue — total closed revenue for the period, with the number up front.
  • Target versus actual — the goal and the gap, expressed as both a figure and a percentage.
  • Pipeline — open deals by stage, with expected close dates.
  • Wins and losses — the two or three deals that moved the number, plus anything lost and why.
  • Forecast — your best estimate for the next period, with the main assumption stated.
  • Blockers — anything stuck on pricing, legal, or a decision-maker.

Leave out the long narrative about every call you made. A sales report is a scoreboard, not a diary. If a deal is interesting, mention it once in wins or losses. Otherwise the reader wants the totals.

Sales Report Template

Copy this block and fill the brackets.

Sales Report — [Week / Month] [Date Range]
Rep: [Your name]

Revenue this period: $[amount]
Target: $[amount]
Variance: [+/- $ and %]

Pipeline by stage:
  - Prospecting: $[amount], [n] deals
  - Proposal: $[amount], [n] deals
  - Negotiation: $[amount], [n] deals

Top wins:
  1. [Account] — $[amount], closed [date], reason: [why]
  2. [Account] — $[amount], closed [date], reason: [why]

Losses:
  1. [Account] — lost to [competitor / no decision], reason: [why]

Forecast next period: $[amount]
Main assumption: [what has to hold true]

Blockers: [pricing / legal / decision delay]

Real Example

Here is a compact weekly report from a real-style SaaS rep.

Sales Report — Week 42 (Oct 13–17, 2026)
Rep: Dana Lee

Revenue this period: $48,000
Target: $60,000
Variance: -$12,000 (-20%)

Pipeline by stage:
  - Prospecting: $120,000, 9 deals
  - Proposal: $80,000, 4 deals
  - Negotiation: $45,000, 2 deals

Top wins:
  1. Northwind Logistics — $30,000, closed Oct 15, reason: faster onboarding beat competitor
  2. Bright Co — $18,000, closed Oct 17, reason: annual prepay discount

Losses:
  1. Atlas Retail — lost to no decision, reason: budget froze until Q1

Forecast next period: $72,000
Main assumption: both negotiation deals close and one proposal advances

Blockers: legal review on Northwind expansion slowing $20k add-on

Notice the report leads with the number. The manager sees the miss in the first three lines, then gets the context. The forecast is tied to a specific assumption, so if it slips, everyone knows which deal to watch.

Common Mistakes

Hiding the bad number. Reps sometimes bury revenue at the bottom or wrap it in prose. Your manager reads top-down. Put the actual versus target first, always.

Reporting activity as outcome. "I made 40 calls" is not a sales report. Calls are inputs. Revenue, pipeline, and wins are outputs. Track activity elsewhere; report results here.

No forecast or a vague one. "Things look good next month" tells nobody anything. A forecast with the key assumption attached is something leadership can plan around.

Copy-pasting the CRM export. Raw CRM dumps are not reports. They shift the work of interpretation onto the reader. Summarize, then attach detail if asked.

Mixing periods. Keep one report to one period. A weekly report that also recaps the quarter confuses the trend. If you need the quarter view, write it as its own section with a clear label.
