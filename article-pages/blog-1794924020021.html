A Practical Playbook for Minutes Decisions

Olivia Thorne
Staff Writer

My calendar said "AI deep dive" for eleven straight weeks. I attended exactly three. The other eight, I opened the doc, skimmed the agenda, and quietly moved the meeting to "next week" — a slot that never arrived. Meanwhile, a competitor shipped a feature I'd flagged as "watch" six months earlier, and my own team's roadmap had drifted two sprints behind without anyone raising a hand.

That's the thing about AI tools for product managers: the watching feels productive. The deciding is what actually moves the roadmap. If you've been circling the same "we should really evaluate this" conversation for a quarter, you don't need another demo. You need a triage habit — thirty minutes, once a week, with a fixed output.

Here's the ritual I use now. It's boring on purpose. That's the point.

Why most AI evaluations stall out

The failure mode is almost always the same. Someone (often you) creates a doc titled "AI Tool Evaluation." It has 14 tabs. Tab 1 is "Landscape." Tab 14 is "Recommendation." Nobody has ever opened tab 14.

The doc grows because the question keeps growing. "Should we use AI for X?" is unanswerable. "Does Tool Y save the support team more than 2 hours a week, measured against the current baseline?" is answerable in about 20 minutes.

So the ritual has one rule: every week produces a decision, not a summary. A decision is one of four things:

  • Adopt — we're rolling it out to a named group by a named date.
  • Test — we're running a 2-week pilot with a pass/fail metric.
  • Park — we've looked; it's not worth revisiting for 6 months.
  • Kill — we tried; it didn't work; here's why.

If you can't pick one of those four at the end of the session, you didn't triage. You browsed.

The 30-minute structure

Block the time. Close Slack. Open three things: your backlog, your last week's triage notes, and a blank doc with four headings (Adopt / Test / Park / Kill). That's it.

Minutes 0–5: Scan what changed

You're looking for signals, not news. Three sources, five minutes total:

  1. Your own team's friction log — the running list of "this took too long" complaints. AI is only worth evaluating when it removes a specific, named friction.
  2. One industry source you trust — a newsletter, a Slack community, a competitor's changelog. One. Not five.
  3. Your inbox — any vendor or internal champion who's pinged you in the last 7 days. Those pings are your queue.

Write down every candidate as a one-liner: "Tool X claims to do Y for Z team." No adjectives. No "promising" or "exciting." Just the claim.

Minutes 5–20: Score against three questions

For each candidate, answer three questions in under 90 seconds. If you can't answer, it defaults to Park.

1. What's the current baseline? Not "we do this manually." How long does it take? How often does it happen? Who does it? If you can't name a number, you can't measure improvement, and you can't defend the decision to your VP later.

2. What's the smallest test that would change your mind? Not a pilot. Not a POC. The smallest thing. "Have one PM use it on next week's user interview notes and compare the summary to their handwritten version." That's a test. "Evaluate for the org" is not.

3. What breaks if we adopt it and it's wrong? This is the question everyone skips. If the answer is "nothing, we just stop using it," the risk is low — move faster. If the answer is "customer data leaves our perimeter" or "the team learns a workflow we can't unlearn," slow down and get legal or security in the room early.

Minutes 20–28: Write the four-line decision

For each candidate that survived, write exactly four lines:

Decision: Test
Owner: [name]
Metric: [specific, measurable, dated]
Revisit: [date]

If you can't fill in all four, you're not ready to decide — and that itself is a decision (Park, revisit next quarter).

Minutes 28–30: Post it where the team reads

Not in the evaluation doc. Not in a Notion page nobody opens. In the same channel where you post your weekly update. One message, four lines per item, tagged to the owner. If you want a template for that update, the weekly status report format I use is built for exactly this kind of short, scannable post.

A worked example

Last quarter, a PM on my team brought me a vendor pitch for an AI tool that auto-generated release notes from Jira tickets. The pitch deck was gorgeous. The demo was smoother than our actual release process.

Here's how the 30 minutes went:

  • Baseline: Our release notes take about 90 minutes per release, twice a month. That's 3 hours a month, one person. Real number, not a guess.
  • Smallest test: Have the PM run the tool on the last three releases and diff the output against what we actually shipped. Pass if it's within 15 minutes of human-level quality and doesn't invent features.
  • What breaks: If the tool hallucinates a feature, we ship a public changelog that lies to customers. That's a real risk — so the test includes a human review step, and we don't automate the publish button.
  • Decision: Test. Owner: the PM. Metric: 3 releases, 15-min quality bar, review step mandatory. Revisit in 3 weeks.

Three weeks later, the tool saved about 40 minutes per release — real, but not the 90 the vendor promised. We adopted it with the human review step. The point isn't that it worked. The point is that we knew within three weeks instead of debating it for a quarter.

What to keep doing yourself

The ritual is about triage, not delegation. Some things should stay human, and the weekly session is where you defend that line.

  • The decision itself. AI can summarize options. It can't own the tradeoff. If you find yourself asking a tool "which one should we pick," stop — that's your job.
  • The relationship with the person affected. If a tool replaces part of someone's workflow, they hear it from you, in a conversation, not from a changelog.
  • The judgment call on risk. Tools don't know your compliance posture, your customer contracts, or which exec will ask which question. You do.

I keep a running "keep human" list next to the four decision headings. Every quarter, I re-read it. Twice, I've moved something off the list because the tool genuinely got better. Most of the time, I haven't.

The compounding effect

The first session feels slow. You'll spend 25 minutes on one candidate and only get through two. Fine. By week four, you'll have a decision log — a running record of what you adopted, tested, parked, and killed, with dates and metrics attached.

That log does two things you can't buy. First, it stops the same conversation from restarting every time a vendor emails you. Second, when your VP asks "what are we doing with AI?" in a quarterly review, you don't have to improvise. You open the log and read four decisions with dates.

The teams that get value from AI tools aren't the ones with the most evaluations. They're the ones with the shortest gap between "we should look at this" and "here's what we decided." Thirty minutes a week closes that gap. The alternative — another calendar block that gets rescheduled — closes nothing.

Pick a recurring slot. This week. Put the four headings in a doc. Bring one candidate. Decide something by minute 30, even if the decision is "park it." Then do it again next week. That's the whole method.
