You Said ‘MVP’. They Heard ‘Sloppy First Version’.

Sarah Jenkins
Staff Writer

You’re in the kickoff meeting, walking the team through the plan for the next big thing. You get to the slide titled “Phase 1: MVP Scope.” You feel good. It’s lean. It’s focused.

Then the questions start.

“So we’re cutting corners on the API?” asks a senior engineer.

“Is this going to feel broken to the user?” the designer follows up, frowning.

From the back, a project manager asks, “When will the real version be ready?”

You’ve lost the room. You used a term you thought meant “a focused, initial version designed for learning,” but your team heard “a cheap, rushed, and low-quality product we’ll have to apologize for.”

The term Minimum Viable Product has become a liability. It’s a repository for everyone’s anxieties about shipping unfinished work. It doesn't align your team; it creates three different, conflicting definitions of "done."

Stop saying MVP. Start talking about what you’re actually trying to do: run an experiment.

Reframe the Release as a Question

Your first release isn't a smaller version of the final product. It’s an instrument designed to answer your biggest, riskiest question. Shifting the language from a noun (the MVP) to a verb (testing a hypothesis) changes everything.

Instead of asking, “What’s the minimum we can build?”

Ask, “What’s the fastest way to learn if we’re even on the right track?”

This simple switch transforms the conversation from one about subtraction and sacrifice to one about focus and learning.

State the Hypothesis and the Non-Negotiables

To make this real for your team, you need to be explicit about two things: the question and the quality bar.

1. The Hypothesis: Frame the work with a clear hypothesis statement.

  • We believe that sales reps
  • will adopt the new lead scoring feature
  • because it saves them from manual data entry.
  • We will know this is true when 75% of our beta group uses the feature on at least five leads within the first week.

Now the goal isn’t to “ship the lead scorer.” The goal is to see if we can get sales reps to change their behavior. The feature is just the tool we're using to find the answer.

2. The Non-Negotiables: An MVP often implies that everything is negotiable, including stability and user experience. This is what terrifies engineers and designers. Address this head-on. Create a short list of what will not be compromised.

  • Production Stability: The code will be clean, tested, and monitored. This is not a prototype.
  • Core User Experience: The workflow must be intuitive and frustration-free. We are reducing scope, not polish.
  • Brand Integrity: The feature will look and feel like our product.

This reassures the team that you’re not asking them to do sloppy work. You’re asking them to do focused work.

Run a ‘Riskiest Assumption Test’ Workshop

Don’t define the scope in a document and present it. Build the scope with your team.

  1. Get the core team together: Product, engineering lead, and design lead.
  2. Write the hypothesis on the board. This is your north star for the session.
  3. Ask the key question: "What is the single biggest assumption we are making?" Maybe it’s a technical assumption (we can integrate with their API). Maybe it's a user value assumption (they even want this).
  4. Brainstorm the smallest possible thing you could ship to test that one assumption. This is not a feature list. It's a list of evidence-gathering components. If the riskiest assumption is "users will give us access to their calendar," you don't need to build the whole scheduling feature. You need to build the most convincing permissions screen and see if anyone clicks "allow."
  5. Cut everything else. Be ruthless. If a proposed component doesn't directly help you validate or invalidate your primary assumption, it goes into the backlog for a future experiment.

By the end of this workshop, you won’t have an "MVP." You'll have a RAT: a Riskiest Assumption Test. It’s a plan that your entire team understands and is aligned on because they helped build it. They aren't just building a feature. They're helping you find an answer.
