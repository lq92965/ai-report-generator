How to Write a Quarterly Report That People Actually Read

Reportify AI Editorial
Staff Writer

A quarterly report is a snapshot of where a team or business stands every three months. If you searched for "how to write a quarterly report," you most likely have a review meeting coming up and a folder full of numbers with no clear way to present them. This page gives you a structure you can fill in this afternoon, a template you can copy, a real example you can read line by line, and the mistakes that make reports get ignored. No theory, no jargon — just the parts that matter to the person reading it.

A quarterly report is different from a monthly one. A month is short enough that you can list what happened. A quarter is long enough that patterns show up, and that is what leadership wants to see: not a log of tasks, but a verdict on whether the plan worked and what should change. If you need the shorter version first, start with our guide on how to write a monthly report, then use this page to scale it up.

What goes in a quarterly report

A good quarterly report answers five questions, in this order. Keep the order and a busy reader can skim the whole thing in under two minutes.

  1. Where we said we would go. Restate the goals set at the start of the quarter. One short line per goal, with the target number attached. If a goal was vague, this is where you find out — and where you fix it for next time.
  2. Where we actually landed. Show results against each goal using numbers. Use the same measures you used last quarter so the comparison is honest. A percentage on its own means very little without the baseline.
  3. Why the gaps happened. Explain the misses and the wins in plain language. A goal missed for a clear reason is far more useful than a goal that was quietly dropped. Own the miss; do not hide it in passive wording.
  4. What changed around us. Note any shift that affected the results — a price change, a lost client, a new hire, a competitor move, a process change. This context is what turns a list of numbers into a story a reader can trust.
  5. What we will do next quarter. Close with three to five commitments. Each one needs an owner and a date. A commitment without either is a wish.

That is the entire skeleton. Everything else is detail. Before you add a section, ask whether it helps the reader make a decision. If it does not, cut it. Reports fail far more often from too much content than from too little.

One more thing about length: aim for two to four pages. Longer than that and people read the summary only. Shorter than that and you probably left out the "why," which is the part that takes the most thinking and earns the most trust.

Quarterly report template

Copy this block into a document and replace the brackets. Keep the headings even when a section is short — a consistent shape makes your reports easier to compare quarter after quarter.

QUARTERLY REPORT - [Team / Department]
Quarter: Q[X] [Year]    Prepared by: [Name]    Date: [Date]

1. GOALS AT START OF QUARTER
- Goal 1: [one line, with target number]
- Goal 2: [one line, with target number]
- Goal 3: [one line, with target number]

2. RESULTS
- Goal 1: [actual] vs [target] - [met / missed / exceeded]
- Goal 2: [actual] vs [target] - [met / missed / exceeded]
- Goal 3: [actual] vs [target] - [met / missed / exceeded]

3. WHAT DROVE THE RESULTS
- Biggest win: [brief cause]
- Biggest miss: [brief cause, owned]

4. CHANGES IN THE PERIOD
- [Market, team, or process change and its effect]

5. NEXT QUARTER COMMITMENTS
- [Action] - owner: [name] - due: [date]
- [Action] - owner: [name] - due: [date]
- [Action] - owner: [name] - due: [date]

If your quarter includes a budget section, keep it separate and link to it rather than pasting a full table into the main body. Our piece on writing a financial report covers that part in detail.

Real example: a customer success team's Q3 report

Here is the same template filled in by a small software team. Notice how plain the language is and how every claim has a number behind it.

QUARTERLY REPORT - Customer Success
Quarter: Q3 2026    Prepared by: Priya N.    Date: Oct 2, 2026

1. GOALS AT START OF QUARTER
- Raise net retention from 104% to 110%
- Cut first-response time from 9h to under 4h
- Publish 12 onboarding help articles

2. RESULTS
- Net retention: 108% vs 110% - missed
- First-response time: 3.4h vs under 4h - met
- Onboarding articles: 14 vs 12 - exceeded

3. WHAT DROVE THE RESULTS
- Biggest miss: two enterprise renewals slipped to Q4, which explains
  most of the retention gap. Both are still active.
- Biggest win: routing tickets by language cut response time
  without adding headcount.

4. CHANGES IN THE PERIOD
- New self-serve setup flow launched in August, so fewer
  setup tickets reached the queue.

5. NEXT QUARTER COMMITMENTS
- Recover the two renewals - owner: Priya - due: Nov 15
- Pilot a health-score alert - owner: Marcus - due: Dec 1
- Refresh the top 5 help articles - owner: Priya - due: Dec 15

This report is short, but it tells a reader everything they need: one goal missed for a named reason, two goals met, one change that helped, and three dated next steps. You could hold a twenty-minute meeting on it and leave with decisions made. That is the standard to aim for. Common mistakes when writing a quarterly report

  • Burying the numbers. Putting results on page three forces readers to hunt. Lead with the scorecard, then explain it.
  • Changing the metrics between quarters. If you swap measurement methods, your trend line is worthless. Keep definitions stable and note any change openly.
  • Explaining without owning. "The number was affected" tells nobody anything. Say what you did or did not do, then say what you will do differently.
  • No owners or dates on next steps. A to-do list without names and deadlines will still be there next quarter, untouched.
  • Copying last quarter and only changing the date. Readers notice. Each quarter deserves fresh thinking about what happened and why.
  • Reporting activity instead of outcomes. "Sent 40 emails" is an activity. "Added six accounts worth $90k" is an outcome. Leadership cares about the second one.

Each of these is easy to fix once you know to look for it. Run through the list before you send the report out; a five-minute check prevents most of the embarrassment.
