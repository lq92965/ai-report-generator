Status Report Examples: Three Samples You Can Copy

Reportify AI Editorial
Staff Writer

A good status report answers a handful of questions in the first ten seconds: what changed, where things stand, and what needs a decision. Most people who search for status report examples are not looking for theory. They want to see a finished report, understand why it works, and adapt it to their own week. That is exactly what this page gives you.

Below you will find three complete samples: a weekly project status report, a monthly report sent to a manager, and a short status update written as an email. Each one follows the same backbone but changes the level of detail to fit the audience. Before the samples, we cover what actually goes into a status report so the examples make sense.

What goes in a status report

Every status report, regardless of length, is built from the same five parts.

Reporting period. State the exact dates at the top. "Week of March 3 to March 7" removes all ambiguity about which days the numbers cover. A reader should never have to guess whether a figure is current.

Overall status. Give one plain-language verdict: on track, at risk, or off track. If you use color, keep it simple (green, amber, red) and always pair it with words, because colors alone fail in email clients and printed copies.

Progress since the last report. List what actually got finished, not what the team worked on. "Shipped the invoice export feature to 40 beta users" is progress. "Worked on the export feature" is activity, and activity is not the same thing. Tie each item to a deliverable or a measurable outcome wherever you can.

Blockers and risks. Name the specific thing standing in the way, who owns resolving it, and by when. A blocker without an owner tends to sit untouched for another week. A risk is slightly different: it is something that might go wrong, so note the odds and the backup plan in one line.

Next steps and decisions needed. List the top two or three actions for the coming period. If you need a decision from the reader, put it here as a direct question with a deadline. Buried requests do not get answered.

One more element is worth adding when the audience is senior: a one-sentence summary at the very top. Executives often read only that line. Write it last, after the rest of the report is finished, so it reflects the real picture. Real example 1: Weekly project status report

This sample is the workhorse format for a project team reporting to a stakeholder each Friday. It stays under one screen and leads with the verdict.

PROJECT STATUS REPORT — Atlas Billing Migration
Reporting period: March 3–7, 2026
Prepared by: Dana Reyes, Project Lead
Overall status: AMBER — scope is stable, but the data cleanup is running three days late.

Summary: The migration will still hit the April 15 go-live date, but only if the
finance team confirms the duplicate-account rules by March 12.

Completed this week:
- Finished field mapping for all 62 legacy columns; signed off by the data lead.
- Migrated and validated 18,400 of 22,000 test customer records (83%).
- Closed 9 of 11 open defects from the February test cycle.

In progress:
- Duplicate-account cleanup (3 days behind; two contractor days added).
- UAT scripts for invoicing, due March 11.

Blockers:
- Duplicate-account rule set is unconfirmed. Owner: Priya Shah (Finance).
  Decision needed by March 12, or the cleanup slips a full week.

Risks:
- Contractor availability drops after March 20. Mitigation: train one internal
  analyst this week to take over validation.

Next steps (March 10–14):
- Complete remaining 3,600 test records and re-run validation.
- Run the first full UAT session with the finance team on March 13.
- Send the revised go-live checklist to stakeholders on March 14.

Decision needed from you: Confirm the duplicate-account rules by March 12.

Why this works: the verdict is on line four, the numbers are specific, every blocker has a name and a date, and the ask at the bottom is a question someone can answer in one reply.

Real example 2: Monthly status report to a manager

A monthly report has a wider lens than a weekly one. The reader cares about trends, budget, and whether the month moved the team toward its goals. Keep the detail lighter and the meaning heavier.

MONTHLY STATUS REPORT — Customer Support Team
Reporting period: February 1–28, 2026
Prepared by: Marcus Lee, Support Manager
Overall status: GREEN

Summary: Ticket volume rose 12% but first-response time improved by 18%;
no headcount change is needed this quarter.

Key results:
- Tickets resolved: 4,182 (target 3,900).
- Median first response: 2h 14m (January: 2h 43m).
- CSAT: 91% (target 88%).
- Cost per ticket: $6.80 (budget $7.10).

What changed this month:
- Published 14 new help-center articles; deflected an estimated 320 tickets.
- Rolled out the updated escalation flow to all three support tiers.
- Hired one part-time agent; training completes March 8.

Issues to watch:
- Chat volume after 6 p.m. is up 30% and is currently covered by overtime.
- Two recurring bugs in the billing portal account for 46 tickets this month.

Plan for March:
- Pilot a late-shift chat schedule before committing to a permanent hire.
- Work with engineering to close the top two billing bugs by March 20.
- Rebaseline the Q2 ticket-volume forecast after the product launch.

No decisions needed this month.

Notice how the numbers tell the story. "Cost per ticket $6.80 against a $7.10 budget" does more work than a paragraph describing careful spending.

Real example 3: Short status update email

Not every report needs a document. When the audience is one person and the update is routine, an email is the right format. This is one of the most requested status update examples because it fits into a busy inbox.

Subject: Status — onboarding redesign (week 22)

Hi Elena,

On track for the May 30 launch. Quick update:

Done: Final wireframes approved by product; the signup flow is now in build.
Next: Engineering finishes the API hookup by Thursday; QA starts Friday.
Heads-up: Legal has not confirmed the revised terms text. I need their sign-off
by May 8 to keep the launch date. I have asked Tom to chase it.

No action needed from you this week.

Thanks,
Dana

The entire message is under 100 words, yet it gives the verdict, progress, the next milestone, and a flag on the one item that could slip. That is the whole job of a short update. For more ways to phrase these lines, see our weekly status report examples and templates.

Common mistakes to avoid

Reporting activity instead of outcomes. "Attended five meetings" tells the reader nothing. Say what the meetings produced.

Burying bad news. If a deadline is at risk, put it in the first two lines. Surprises at the end of a report destroy trust.

Vague status labels. "Almost done" means different things to different people. Use a fixed scale and define what each level means.

Missing owners and dates. Every blocker needs a person and a deadline. Without them, the next report will list the same blocker again.

Too much detail for the audience. A weekly team report can list individual tasks. A monthly report to a director should group them into results. Match the granularity to the reader.

No ask. If you need something, say so directly. A status report that never requests a decision wastes the one moment you have the reader's attention.
