The 15-Minute Monday Triage: How PMs Set the Week Before It Sets Them

David Sterling
Staff Writer

Monday, 9:04 a.m. You open your laptop to 47 unread Slack messages, three "quick question" DMs that are not quick, and a calendar that looks like a game of Tetris played by someone who hates you. By 11 a.m. you've answered everyone else's questions and answered none of your own. The week is already running you.

The fix isn't a better to-do app. It's a 15-minute ritual on Monday morning that decides what the week is about before anyone else decides for you. I call it the Monday triage. I've run it for two years across three product teams, and it's the single highest-leverage 15 minutes in my week.

Here's the exact sequence.

Why Monday morning, not Sunday night

Sunday-night planning is a trap. You're tired, you're guessing at what the week holds, and you're doing it in the worst possible mood. Monday morning gives you the one thing Sunday doesn't: fresh information. Overnight messages, weekend deploys, and whatever broke at 6 a.m. in a timezone you don't live in — that's all real data now.

Do the triage before you open Slack. I mean it. The triage is a filter; if you run it after the inbox, you're filtering someone else's priorities.

Step 1: Dump the open loops (4 minutes)

Open a blank doc. Not your task manager — a blank doc. Write down every open loop you're carrying: the decision you owe legal, the spec that's half-written, the customer call you haven't scheduled, the metric you promised to check. Don't organize. Don't prioritize. Just dump.

Aim for 15–25 items. If you're under 10, you're forgetting things. If you're over 40, you're carrying work that isn't yours — flag that as its own problem.

This step exists because open loops live in your head as ambient anxiety, not as a list. Getting them onto a page converts vague dread into countable objects. Countable objects can be triaged. Dread cannot.

Step 2: Sort into three buckets (5 minutes)

Now mark each item with one of three letters:

  • D — Decide. Something only you can resolve. A call, a trade-off, a yes/no.
  • Dl — Delegate. Someone else can do this, or should. Your job is to hand it off with enough context.
  • Df — Defer. Real, but not this week. Park it with a date.

The trap here is marking everything D. If more than five items are D, you're hoarding. Most "only I can do this" items are actually "only I have decided to do this." Hand them off.

The Defer bucket is the one people skip, and it's the one that saves you. A deferred item with a date stops haunting you. A deferred item without a date becomes a D next Monday, and the Monday after that.

Step 3: Pick the week's one outcome (3 minutes)

Look at your D items. Pick the single one that, if it shipped by Friday, would make the week a win regardless of what else happened. Write it at the top of the doc in one sentence.

This is your anchor. When Wednesday's fire drill hits, you'll ask: does this protect or threaten the anchor? If it threatens it, the fire drill gets a smaller response than it's demanding.

One outcome, not three. Three outcomes is zero outcomes with extra steps.

Step 4: Send one short note (3 minutes)

Now — and only now — open your communication tools. Send a single short message to your team and your key stakeholder. Three lines:

  1. This week's focus: the anchor, in plain language.
  2. What I need from you: specific asks, with names.
  3. What I'm not doing this week: one or two deferred items, named, so nobody is surprised.

That third line is the one most PMs skip, and it's the one that buys you the most goodwill. Telling people what you're not doing is a form of respect. It prevents the silent disappointment that builds when people assume you're on something you've quietly deprioritized.

What this replaces

The Monday triage isn't a new meeting. It's a replacement for three things you're probably already doing badly:

  • The reactive inbox sweep. You'll still do it, but after the triage, so you're triaging messages against a stated priority instead of letting them become the priority.
  • The vague weekly plan. Most weekly plans are lists of activities. The triage produces one outcome and a set of decisions. Activities get done; outcomes get defended.
  • The Sunday-night spiral. Once you've done this for a few weeks, your brain learns that Monday morning will handle it. The 2 a.m. "did I forget something" wake-ups get quieter.

When to skip it

Skip the triage on weeks when you already know the answer — a launch week, a board-meeting week, a week where the anchor is obvious and pre-committed. The triage is for weeks where the priority is genuinely unclear. Running it as a ritual when you don't need it turns it into theater.

The other time to skip it: when you're so underwater that 15 minutes feels impossible. That's exactly when you need it most. Do a five-minute version — dump, pick one anchor, send one note. The abbreviated version still beats the alternative.

The compounding effect

The first Monday triage takes 20 minutes and feels like busywork. By week four it takes 10, and you'll notice something: your Fridays are calmer. Not because less happened, but because more of what happened was the thing you chose.

The week will always try to set you. Fifteen minutes on Monday is how you set it first.
