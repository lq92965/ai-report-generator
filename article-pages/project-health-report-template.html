Project Health Report Template: See Trouble Before It Costs You

Reportify AI Editorial
Staff Writer

A project health report answers a different question from a status update. A status update says what happened this week. A health report says whether the project is still likely to succeed, and it does so while there is still time to change the outcome. That difference matters. Most projects do not fail in one dramatic week; they drift, one small variance at a time, until the deadline is close and the options are gone. A health report catches that drift early.

The trick is to measure health, not activity. A project can have busy teams, full meeting calendars, and a pile of completed tasks while quietly heading for a missed deadline. Health means the vital signs — scope, schedule, budget, quality, and risk — are all within a tolerable range at the same time. This article gives you a one-page template that tracks those signals, a filled example, and the mistakes that make health reports useless. If you need the broader writing method first, the guide to writing a project status report covers cadence and audience.

What goes in a project health report

A good health report is a dashboard with a short explanation attached. Six sections cover it:

  • Overall rating. One word — green, amber, or red — with a one-line reason. The rating is the verdict; everything after it is evidence.
  • Vital signs table. Scope, schedule, budget, quality, and resources, each rated and each backed by a number.
  • Change since last report. What moved, in which direction, and whether the movement matters. A single-line change often tells more than the current rating.
  • Top risks. The two or three risks with the highest chance of hurting the project, each with a probability, an impact, and an owner.
  • Decisions needed. Anything the reader must approve to keep the project healthy, with a date by which the decision is needed.
  • Recovery actions. For anything amber or red, the specific step being taken, who owns it, and when it should show an effect.

The rating scale needs a fixed meaning or it becomes decoration. Green means the project will hit its targets with the current plan. Amber means a target is at risk but recoverable without changing the goal. Red means a target will be missed unless something changes. Write those definitions once at the top of the template so every reader reads the colors the same way.

Keep it to one page. A health report that runs to ten pages will not be read during the week it could still help.

Project health report template

Copy this block and replace the brackets.

PROJECT HEALTH REPORT
Project: [project name]
Reporting date: [date]
Prepared by: [name, role]
Overall rating: [GREEN / AMBER / RED] — [one-line reason]

RATING KEY: Green = on target. Amber = at risk, recoverable. Red = target will be missed without change.

VITAL SIGNS
Area       | Rating | Evidence
Scope      | [color] | [e.g. 2 change requests pending, +8% scope]
Schedule   | [color] | [e.g. milestone 3 is 9 days late, 62% of work done]
Budget     | [color] | [e.g. 410k spent of 500k, 82% used at 70% completion]
Quality    | [color] | [e.g. 14 open defects, 3 severity-1]
Resources  | [color] | [e.g. 2 roles unfilled for 5 weeks]

CHANGE SINCE LAST REPORT
[Area] moved from [color] to [color] because [specific reason].

TOP RISKS
Risk | Chance | Impact | Owner | Next step
[1]  | [H/M/L] | [H/M/L] | [name] | [action]
[2]  | [H/M/L] | [H/M/L] | [name] | [action]

DECISIONS NEEDED
1. [Decision] — needed by [date], owner [name].

RECOVERY ACTIONS
1. [Action] for [area] — owner [name], effect expected by [date].

Real project health report example

Here is the structure filled in for a fictional product build.

PROJECT HEALTH REPORT
Project: Atlas Mobile App v2
Reporting date: October 18, 2026
Prepared by: Sam Okafor, Delivery Lead
Overall rating: AMBER — the launch date is at risk because the API integration is nine days behind.

RATING KEY: Green = on target. Amber = at risk, recoverable. Red = target will be missed without change.

VITAL SIGNS
Area       | Rating | Evidence
Scope      | AMBER  | 3 change requests pending, adding an estimated 10% scope
Schedule   | AMBER  | Milestone 3 (API integration) 9 days late; 62% of build complete
Budget     | GREEN  | 410k spent of 500k; spend is in line with progress
Quality    | GREEN  | 11 open defects, none severity-1
Resources  | GREEN  | All planned roles filled; contractor starts Oct 21

CHANGE SINCE LAST REPORT
Schedule moved from GREEN to AMBER: the payments vendor delayed sandbox access by two weeks.

TOP RISKS
Risk                         | Chance | Impact | Owner    | Next step
Payments sandbox still late  | HIGH   | HIGH   | Sam      | Escalate to vendor account manager Oct 19
Scope creep from 3 requests  | MED    | MED    | Lena     | Freeze non-critical requests until Nov 3

DECISIONS NEEDED
1. Approve 25k for a temporary integration contractor — needed by Oct 22, owner Sam Okafor.

RECOVERY ACTIONS
1. Parallelize API work with two engineers — owner Sam Okafor, effect expected by Oct 28.
2. Defer the loyalty-points feature to v2.1 — owner Lena Park, decision by Oct 22.

Why this example works

The rating appears in the first line with a reason attached, so nobody has to infer the verdict from a table. Every color is backed by a number — nine days late, 62% complete, 11 defects — which makes the judgment checkable. And the report never stops at a problem: each risk has an owner, and each amber signal has a recovery action with a date.

The "change since last report" line is the most underrated part. It tells a reader that schedule slipped this week because of a vendor, not that it has always been late. That context changes what the reader decides to do. A project sitting at amber because of a fresh problem deserves a different response from one that has been amber for six weeks. Without the trend line, both look identical on the page, and the reader has no way to tell a recoverable stumble from a slow collapse.

The ratings key matters for the same reason. Two people can look at the same project and honestly disagree about whether it is green or amber. Writing down what each color means removes the argument. Green is not "everything is fine," and red is not "the project is doomed." They are shorthand for specific, testable conditions. When the whole team shares that shorthand, a health report becomes a fast way to agree on where the project stands instead of another document to debate.

Common mistakes in a project health report

  • Reporting only green. If every area is always green, either the project is extraordinary or the ratings are not honest. Readers stop trusting the color.
  • Rating without evidence. "Budget: amber" tells a reader nothing. Add the number that produced the rating.
  • Confusing health with progress. A project can be 80% complete and still unhealthy. Progress describes where you are; health describes whether you will finish well.
  • Skipping the trend. A single snapshot hides direction. Always show what changed since the last report.
  • Listing risks with no owner. A risk nobody owns will not be managed. Assign a person to every line.
  • Burying decisions needed. If the reader must act, put that request near the top and give it a deadline.
