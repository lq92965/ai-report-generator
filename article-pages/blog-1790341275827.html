You're Triaging Slack All Day. You Need a Communication Protocol.

Sarah Jenkins
Staff Writer

It’s 9:17 AM. You’ve been at your desk for less than an hour, but you’ve already been pulled in ten different directions. A designer DMs you a “quick question” about a spec you finalized last week. A sales leader tags you in a public channel asking for the ETA on a feature that isn’t even on the roadmap. Your engineering lead is asking in another channel if the bug report from customer support is a P0 or P1.

You feel less like a product manager and more like a human router, frantically redirecting information packets before they collide. Your day is a series of context switches, and the deep work you planned—analyzing that user research, drafting the Q3 strategy—is a distant dream.

This isn’t a personal time management failure. It’s a system failure. When there are no explicit rules for communication, the default becomes a firehose aimed directly at you. You need to stop triaging the chaos and start designing the system. You need a communication protocol.

This isn't about adding bureaucracy. It's about creating clarity. A simple, shared agreement on where different types of conversations happen frees everyone from the tyranny of the urgent.

The Four-Tier Communication Stack

Think of your team's communication as a stack with four distinct layers, each with a specific job. Your goal is to get the right conversation into the right layer.

Tier 1: The Real-Time Layer (Slack, Teams)

This is for high-velocity, low-permanence communication. It’s for things that are happening right now.

  • Use for: Urgent, time-sensitive blockers ("The CI/CD pipeline is broken!"), quick, non-binding clarifications ("What’s the link to that Figma file again?"), and team celebrations.
  • Do NOT use for: Making decisions, debating complex tradeoffs, submitting new feature requests, or asking questions that will need to be referenced next quarter.
  • The Rule: If a conversation requires more than three back-and-forths or a "let me think about that," it's time to move it up the stack.

Tier 2: The Work Layer (Jira, Asana, Linear)

This is where the work lives. Communication here should be tied directly to a specific ticket, task, or bug.

  • Use for: Questions about acceptance criteria, implementation details, bug reproduction steps, and status updates on a specific piece of work.
  • The Rule: The ticket is the single source of truth for the execution of the work. All commentary should live there, linked to the relevant spec doc. This creates a clean audit trail.

Tier 3: The Knowledge Layer (Confluence, Notion, Coda)

This is your permanent record. It’s the home for the "why" behind the work. This layer is asynchronous and built for thoughtful consumption, not instant reaction.

  • Use for: PRDs, strategy memos, decision logs, user research summaries, and meeting notes with clear action items.
  • The Rule: A decision is not a decision until it is documented here. You can reference it from Slack or Jira, but this is the canon. It’s where you send stakeholders when they ask, "Remind me why we chose this option?"

Tier 4: The High-Bandwidth Layer (Meetings)

Meetings are the most expensive tool in your arsenal. They should be reserved for situations where asynchronous communication is inefficient.

  • Use for: High-stakes strategic alignment, collaborative problem-solving on a complex issue, and building team rapport. These are activities that benefit from the nuance of real-time human interaction.
  • The Rule: No agenda, no meeting. Every meeting must have a stated goal and result in a documented outcome that gets stored in Tier 3.

How to Roll This Out Without Sounding Like a Robot

You can't just drop a link to this article in Slack and expect everyone to change their habits. You have to implement the protocol with a human touch.

First, get buy-in from your core team—your engineering lead and designer. Frame it as a way to protect everyone's focus and reduce noise.

Next, write it down. Create a simple one-page document in your knowledge hub outlining the four tiers and the rules for each. Don't make it a 10-page manifesto.

Then, start gently redirecting traffic. When someone DMs you a feature request, don't just ignore them. Say, "This is a great idea. So I don't lose it, could you add it to our intake form here? That's where we track all new requests." When a debate breaks out in Slack, say, "Lots of good points here. This feels too complex for Slack. I've started a doc here to capture these thoughts so we can make a proper decision."

You have to model the behavior you want to see. Be disciplined about where you post. Soon, the team will see the benefit of having their questions answered in the right place and their focus time protected.

The goal isn't to eliminate communication; it's to improve its signal-to-noise ratio. By building these clear channels, you reclaim your calendar and your brain space for the work that matters. You can finally stop being a router and start being a product leader.
