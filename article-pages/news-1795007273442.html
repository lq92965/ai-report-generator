The Sprint Velocity Is Now a Body Count

Sarah Jenkins
Staff Writer

The standup starts at 9:15. By 9:22, the engineering manager has read twelve ticket numbers aloud in a flat voice, and the room has the particular silence of a hospital corridor. Nobody is talking about the payments bug that surfaced on Tuesday. Nobody is talking about the on-call engineer who logged off at 3 a.m. and hasn't been seen since. They are talking about points. Forty-two of them, against a commitment of fifty.

The velocity chart is pinned to the wall like a war memorial, and every two weeks someone adds a column of numbers to it. This is the ritual we've built: a planning meeting that assigns weights to human labor, a burndown chart that tracks the decline of a small force, and a retrospective where the survivors are asked to reflect on what went wrong.

I've sat through enough of these to know the language. "We didn't hit our velocity." "The team's velocity is down this quarter." "Can we get velocity back up?" The word does a lot of work. It makes a number sound like a property of the team, something they possess or lack, rather than a number the team was assigned by people who were guessing.

Here's what the number actually measures. It measures how many tickets the team agreed to call "done" in a two-week window, weighted by a Fibonacci sequence that nobody has ever defended in a meeting. It does not measure whether the feature works. It does not measure whether the customer is happy. It measures whether the team's self-estimate matched the team's self-report. That's it. That's the whole instrument.

And yet the number has become the thing managers manage. It shows up in board decks. It shows up in performance reviews. It shows up in the conversation about whether to hire another engineer. The velocity chart is not a measurement of the work; it's a measurement of the team's compliance with a ritual, and like any ritual it can be gamed by anyone who understands it. Story points inflate. Tickets get split into smaller tickets. A one-day task becomes three tickets of one-third of a day each, and the velocity goes up, and everyone nods, and nothing has changed except the number.

The sharper problem is what the number does to the people underneath it. When velocity is the metric, the fastest way to raise it is to stop doing the work that doesn't generate tickets. The documentation. The code review. The conversation with the customer that prevents the next three bugs. The thirty minutes spent reading the stack trace instead of shipping a patch. All of that is invisible to the chart, so it gets squeezed out, and the chart goes up, and the system gets more fragile, and the next sprint is harder.

This is not a new observation. It's the oldest observation in management, dressed up in Agile clothing. Goodhart's law is older than the standup. But the specific form it takes in software is worth naming, because the velocity chart is one of the few metrics in modern business that gets displayed on a wall like a scoreboard, and the people being scored are in the room.

There's a moment in every sprint planning where someone says "that feels like an eight" and everyone else nods, and the ticket becomes an eight, and the total goes up by eight. The number is a feeling. It's a feeling that has been converted into a commitment, and the commitment has been converted into a measurement, and the measurement has been converted into a judgment about whether the team is good. Four conversions, each one losing fidelity, and at the end of it a manager is looking at a chart and deciding whether to be disappointed.

The teams that do well under this system are not the ones doing the best work. They're the ones who have learned to estimate generously and split tickets aggressively and never let a hard problem sit in a single ticket for three sprints. They're the ones who understand that the chart is theater and have learned to put on a good show. The teams that struggle are the ones who take the estimates seriously, who put the real work in the real tickets, and who watch the number sag when the work turns out to be harder than the feeling suggested.

I don't think the fix is to abolish estimation. Estimation is useful; it forces a conversation about scope. The fix is to stop putting the number on the wall. Stop reading it in the standup. Stop treating it as a property of the team. The velocity chart is a planning tool that has been promoted to a performance metric, and that promotion is the mistake. A number that's supposed to help you decide what to take on next sprint is not a number that should decide whether you're doing a good job.

The standup ends at 9:29. The manager writes the number on the whiteboard and caps the marker. Somebody on the call unmutes to say the payments bug is still open. The manager says they'll look at it after the sprint review. The sprint review is on Thursday. The chart on the wall has a new column, and the column is short, and everyone in the room knows what short means.
